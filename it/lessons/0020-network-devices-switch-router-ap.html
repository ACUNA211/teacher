Network devices: who forwards what, and how

About 10 minutes. Objective 2.5 is the hardware in the wiring closet.[1] Today is the first half: the boxes that move traffic inside a building (switch, router, access point) plus the two that sit beside them (patch panel, firewall). The win: for each box, say what it looks at to make its decision.

Why this matters at work

When a device can't reach the cloud sign-in page or check in with device management, the cause can sit in any box on the path. "Can reach the printer next to it but nothing else" points at the router or firewall; "nothing at all on this one desk" points at the cable, patch panel or switch port. Knowing what each box does tells you which team to hand the ticket to.

One question per box: what does it look at?

DeviceLooks atWhat it does
Hub (obsolete)NothingRepeats every frame out every port. More traffic means more collisions, so performance falls apart under load. Switches replaced hubs.[2]
SwitchMAC addressLearns which device is on which port and sends each frame only to the port where the destination lives.[1]
RouterIP addressForwards packets between different IP networks (subnets). It's the default gateway from lesson 0001: the way off your own subnet.[3]
Access pointMAC addressBridges Wi-Fi onto the wired network. Like a switch, it decides by destination MAC whether a frame goes out the radio or the cable.[1] It doesn't route.[4]
Patch panelNothingA passive block of jacks. Each desk's cable run is punched down on the back (lesson 0019); short patch cables in front connect it to a switch port.[1] No power, no decisions.
FirewallRules: ports, and on some, applicationsAllows or blocks traffic against its rule list, for example by port number; newer ones can also filter by application.[1] Often built into the router at a small site.

The "wireless router" in a home or small office is several of these in one box: router, small switch, access point and firewall.[4] In a bigger building they're separate devices, which is why the exam treats them separately.

Unmanaged vs. managed switches

An unmanaged switch is plug and play: no settings, nothing to log in to. A managed switch can be configured and monitored.[1] What that buys you:

  • VLANs: put each port in a network segment (lesson 0016).
  • Traffic prioritization (QoS): voice and video calls go first.
  • Redundancy: spare links between switches without loops.
  • Port mirroring and monitoring: copy a port's traffic for troubleshooting (compare the tap in lesson 0019).[3]

Rule of thumb: if anyone needs VLANs, the switch must be managed.

Memory hook

Switch and access point: MAC (same network). Router: IP (between networks). Firewall: rules. Hub and patch panel: nothing.

Try it (2 minutes)

To send anything off your subnet, your PC needs the router's MAC address, because the switch only understands MACs. Windows keeps a table of the MACs it has learned (the ARP cache). In PowerShell:

ipconfig | findstr /i "gateway"
Get-NetNeighbor -AddressFamily IPv4 -State Reachable,Stale

Example output (trimmed; yours will differ):

   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.0.2.1

ifIndex IPAddress      LinkLayerAddress   State
------- ---------      ----------------   -----
12      192.0.2.1      02-00-5E-10-00-01  Reachable
12      192.0.2.40     02-00-5E-10-00-28  Stale

Find the gateway's IP in the list: the LinkLayerAddress next to it is the router's MAC.[5] Every frame you send to the internet carries that MAC as its destination, and that's what the switch uses to pick the port. (arp -a shows the same table.)

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A small office's wired network crawls whenever anyone copies a large file. In the closet you find an old hub. Why does swapping in a switch help?

A hub repeats every frame out every port, so one big copy floods every device and collisions pile up. A switch learns where each MAC address lives and sends the frame only to that port. Handing out addresses is DHCP's job; blocking ports is a firewall's.

A new branch needs guest devices kept apart from staff on separate VLANs, and the team wants to mirror one port for troubleshooting. Which do you order?

VLANs and port mirroring are settings, and only a managed switch has settings. An unmanaged switch is plug and play with nothing to configure; a hub can't even keep traffic apart; an access point doesn't replace the wired switch.

A desktop can print to the printer on its own subnet but can't reach anything on other subnets or the internet. Which device's address is most likely wrong in its settings?

Traffic on the same subnet goes straight through the switch, which is why the printer works. Anything off the subnet must go through the router, so a wrong default gateway breaks exactly that. Switches, access points and patch panels don't appear in a PC's IP settings.

Staff can browse websites, but a new internal app listening on TCP 8443 times out from every office PC. On the server itself it works. What most likely needs a change?

Web traffic works, so cables, switches and Wi-Fi are fine. Only one port number fails, and filtering by port number is exactly what a firewall does: it needs a rule allowing TCP 8443 to that server. An unmanaged switch has no settings, and a patch panel makes no decisions.

From memory: which devices decide by MAC address, which by IP address, which by rules, and which decide nothing? (then click to check)

MAC: switch, access point. IP: router. Rules (ports, applications): firewall. Nothing: hub (repeats everything) and patch panel (passive jacks).

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

You need to find which unlabeled cable in the closet goes to one desk, then check that it's wired correctly. Which two tools, in which order? (lesson 0019, yesterday)

First the tone generator and probe to find the cable (tone on the wall jack, probe at the patch panel). Then the cable tester to check every pin end to end. A toner finds a wire; it doesn't prove it's good.

Where does a domain's DMARC policy live in DNS, and what are its three policy values, from gentlest to strictest? (lesson 0013, 7 days ago)

In a TXT record at _dmarc. plus the domain. Policies: p=none (just report), p=quarantine (send failures to junk), p=reject (refuse them). Start at none and tighten.

Which three 2.4 GHz channels don't overlap, and why does that matter when you place access points? (lesson 0006, 14 days ago)

Channels 1, 6 and 11. Neighboring access points on overlapping channels interfere with each other, so you spread them across those three.
