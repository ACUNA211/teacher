Spinning or solid: HDDs, SSDs and how they connect

About 10 minutes. Memory is done; now the place data lives when the power is off. Today: the two kinds of drive (hard disk and solid-state) and the three ways they talk to the computer (SATA, SAS, NVMe).[1] The win: read a drive's spec line and say how fast it can be and where it belongs. (M.2 and mSATA slots, flash cards and optical drives come in the next lesson.)

Why this matters at work

"Sign-in takes forever" tickets often look like an account problem. On an older laptop the cause is often the drive: a slow spinning disk loading the profile and apps. Swapping a hard drive for an SSD can greatly improve the whole system's speed,[1] and a one-line check tells you which kind is inside before you start resetting anything.

1. Two kinds of drive

Hard disk drive (HDD)Solid-state drive (SSD)
Stores data onMagnetic spinning platters, read by a moving armFlash memory chips, no moving parts
SpeedLimited by how fast the platter spins and the arm movesMuch faster, especially for many small reads (booting, opening apps)
StrengthLots of capacity for little moneySpeed, silence, survives bumps and drops
WeaknessMoving parts wear out and hate being droppedCosts more per terabyte

That's why big, cheap storage (backups, file servers) still uses hard drives, and almost every new laptop ships with an SSD.[1]

Spindle speed is how fast an HDD's platters spin, in rpm: 5,400 (cheap laptops), 7,200 (desktops), 10,000 and 15,000 (servers). Faster spins mean less waiting for the data to come under the head, but more heat and power.[2][3] An SSD has no rpm at all.

Form factors: 3.5-inch drives go in desktops, 2.5-inch in laptops (and many servers).[1] A 2.5-inch SATA SSD is the same shape as a 2.5-inch hard drive, which is what makes the laptop upgrade easy (lesson 0023).

A hybrid drive (SSHD) puts a small SSD cache in front of a hard drive: the files you use most load from flash, the rest from the platters.

2. Three ways to connect

InterfaceWhat it isTop speedWhere
SATAThe standard drive cable (lesson 0042), using the AHCI protocol, which was designed for hard drives6 Gbps, about 600 MB/sDesktops, laptops; HDDs and cheaper SSDs
SAS (Serial Attached SCSI)Enterprise version of the idea: built for servers, high-rpm drives and hot-swap bays12 Gbps and upServers, storage arrays
NVMe over PCIeA protocol made for flash, talking straight over PCI Express lanes (no SATA in the way)Several GB/sFast SSDs in laptops, desktops, servers

The big idea: a fast SSD on SATA is held back by an interface built for spinning disks. AHCI has one command queue, 32 commands deep; NVMe allows thousands of queues, each up to about 65,000 commands.[1][4] That's why "SSD" alone doesn't tell you the speed: a SATA SSD tops out near 600 MB/s, an NVMe SSD goes several times faster.

Rule of thumb

HDD = cheap capacity, SSD = speed. SATA for everyday drives (≈600 MB/s), SAS for servers, NVMe over PCIe for the fastest SSDs. Read both parts of a spec line: what kind of drive, and how it connects.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. In Windows PowerShell, list the drives:
    Get-PhysicalDisk | Format-Table FriendlyName, MediaType, BusType, Size
  2. Read the two middle columns. MediaType says what it is (HDD, SSD, or Unspecified if Windows can't tell). BusType says how it's attached (SATA, SAS, NVMe, USB).[5]
  3. No PowerShell handy? Search the Start menu for Defragment and Optimize Drives: the Media type column shows "Hard disk drive" or "Solid state drive".
A laptop shows MediaType HDD, BusType SATA, and its user says sign-in is painfully slow. What would you suggest, and what speed limit would the new drive face on that same connector? (then click to check)

Replace the spinning hard drive with an SSD: the biggest single speed-up for an old machine. A 2.5-inch SATA SSD fits the same bay, but it's capped near 600 MB/s by SATA. (If the laptop has an NVMe slot, an NVMe SSD would go faster; slots are next lesson.)

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

An older laptop with a 5,400 rpm hard drive takes minutes to boot and sign in. RAM is fine. What's the best upgrade?

An SSD has no moving parts, so the many small reads at boot and sign-in are far faster than on any spinning disk.[1] A 7,200 rpm drive helps only a little; RAM is already fine; defragmenting doesn't fix a slow drive type.

A server's spec sheet lists 2.5-inch 15,000 rpm hard drives at 12 Gbps in hot-swap bays. Which interface is that?

High-rpm server drives at 12 Gbps in hot-swap bays point to SAS (Serial Attached SCSI). SATA tops out at 6 Gbps, NVMe is for SSDs over PCIe, and eSATA is an external cable.

Two 1 TB SSDs: one reads at about 550 MB/s, the other at several GB/s. What explains the gap?

About 550 MB/s is the ceiling of SATA (6 Gbps, AHCI). NVMe over PCIe skips SATA and was designed for flash, so it reaches several GB/s. SSDs don't spin, and parity is a RAM feature (lesson 0048).

Get-PhysicalDisk shows a drive with MediaType SSD and BusType SATA. What is it?

MediaType says what it is (SSD), BusType says how it's attached (SATA). So it's a SATA SSD: no moving parts, but capped near 600 MB/s. An NVMe drive would show BusType NVMe; a USB drive, BusType USB.

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A switch's fiber module takes a small connector, used in transmit/receive pairs, with a latching clip. Which connector? (lesson 0046, 3 days ago)

LC: the small-form connector with a clip, usually as a duplex pair. SC is the larger square push-pull one; ST twists on like a bayonet.

A new SATA drive is screwed in, but the PC doesn't see it. Which two cables should you check, and where does each go? (lesson 0042, 7 days ago)

The 7-pin SATA data cable to the motherboard, and the 15-pin SATA power cable from the power supply. A drive needs both.

You need to find which patch-panel port a user's wall jack runs to, without unplugging everything. Which tool? (lesson 0019, 30 days ago)

A tone generator and probe (toner): put the tone on the cable at the wall jack, then sweep the probe across the patch panel until it beeps.
