Big, medium, tiny: motherboard form factors

About 10 minutes. The last few lessons covered what plugs into a computer: memory, drives, RAID. Now the board they all plug into. A motherboard's form factor is a standard for its size, the position of its mounting holes, its rear I/O panel and its power connector.[3] Messer's 3.5 video covers three: ATX, micro-ATX and ITX.[1] The win: given a case and a board, say whether it fits, and pick a size for a job.

Why this matters at work

When you order a replacement board, a new case or a compact PC for a front desk, the form factor decides what physically fits and how many add-in cards you can use later. A tiny PC is quiet and takes no desk space, but it may have no free slot for that extra network card or capture card someone asks for next year.

1. The three sizes

Form factorSize (about)Expansion slotsTypical use
ATX305 × 244 mm (12 × 9.6 in)Most: up to 7Full-size towers, workstations, gaming PCs
Micro-ATX (mATX, µATX)244 × 244 mm (9.6 × 9.6 in)Fewer: up to 4Office desktops, smaller towers
Mini-ITX170 × 170 mm (6.7 × 6.7 in)Usually 1Small form factor PCs, media and kiosk boxes

Sizes and typical slot counts from form-factor guides; slot counts vary by board, so check the spec sheet.[3] ATX was standardized by Intel in 1995; micro-ATX is a shorter board with a similar layout.[1] Memory hook: micro-ATX is ATX with the bottom cut off, so it's square. You may also see E-ATX (extended, larger than ATX, for servers and high-end desktops) and even smaller ITX sizes (Nano-, Pico-ITX) used in embedded systems.

2. Why a small board fits a big case

Micro-ATX and Mini-ITX reuse a subset of the ATX mounting-hole positions and the same rear I/O panel position.[2][3] So:

  • A smaller board fits a larger case: Mini-ITX or micro-ATX screws into an ATX tower using the matching standoffs.
  • A larger board does not fit a smaller case: an ATX board is simply too big for a Mini-ITX case.

Cases list the board sizes they support (for example, "ATX / mATX / Mini-ITX"). Read that line before ordering.

Rule of thumb

ATX = big, most slots. Micro-ATX = square, fewer slots, fits ATX cases. Mini-ITX = tiny, one slot, fits almost anything. Small board → big case: yes. Big board → small case: no.

Try it (3 minutes)

  1. On a Windows desktop, in PowerShell: Get-CimInstance Win32_BaseBoard | Select-Object Manufacturer, Product. Product is usually the board's model.[4] (No PowerShell handy? msinfo32 shows BaseBoard Manufacturer and BaseBoard Product.)
  2. Search the model name and find its form factor on the maker's spec page.
  3. On a laptop or many brand-name office PCs you'll get a vendor part number instead: those use proprietary boards that only fit that model's chassis.
Before checking: which of the three would you guess a typical compact office desktop uses, and why? (then click to check)

Often micro-ATX (or a proprietary board of similar size): smaller and cheaper than ATX, with a few slots left over. The tiniest "mini PCs" use Mini-ITX or smaller proprietary boards.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A reception desk needs a very small, quiet PC. It will never need an add-in card. Which form factor fits best?

Mini-ITX is the smallest of the three (about 17 cm square) and usually has one expansion slot, which is fine when no cards are needed.[3] Small form factors suit single-purpose machines.[2]

A technician has a new micro-ATX board and an empty ATX mid-tower case. Will the board mount in the case?

Micro-ATX uses a subset of the ATX mounting-hole positions and the same I/O panel position, so it screws straight into an ATX case.[3] Smaller board into bigger case works; the reverse doesn't.

A user's compact PC has a Mini-ITX case. The cheapest replacement board in stock is ATX. What do you tell them?

An ATX board is about 30 × 24 cm; a Mini-ITX case is built for a board about 17 × 17 cm. A bigger board never fits a smaller case. Order a Mini-ITX board (or a bigger case).

An office PC needs a graphics card and two other add-in cards, in the smallest standard board that still has room. Which form factor?

Three cards need three slots. Micro-ATX offers up to four, in a smaller board than ATX.[3] Mini-ITX usually has only one; ATX and E-ATX work but are bigger than needed.

From memory: the three form factors from largest to smallest, roughly how many slots each has, and the fit rule. (then click to check)

ATX (12 × 9.6 in, up to 7 slots) → micro-ATX (9.6 × 9.6 in, up to 4) → Mini-ITX (6.7 × 6.7 in, usually 1). Smaller boards fit bigger cases; bigger boards don't fit smaller cases.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

You have four 4 TB drives. How much usable space does RAID 5 give, and how many drives can fail? And why can't RAID 1 bring back a deleted folder? (lesson 0052, 1 day ago)

RAID 5: 12 TB (one drive's worth goes to parity), survives 1 failure. RAID 1 writes the delete to both drives at once: RAID is not a backup. Restore from a backup or version history.

A fiber patch cable has small connectors with a latch, in a transmit/receive pair. Which connector is it, and what's the trap with a green connector? (lesson 0046, 7 days ago)

LC (small, latching, used in pairs). Green = APC (angled polish): never mate it with a blue UPC connector.

Before swapping a laptop's HDD for an SSD on a BitLocker-encrypted PC, what should you check and do? (lesson 0023, 30 days ago)

Check the form factor and interface the laptop takes (2.5-inch SATA or M.2), make sure the recovery key is available, and suspend BitLocker before hardware or firmware changes so you aren't stuck at the recovery screen.
