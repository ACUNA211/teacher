How to take it

No scrolling back to the lessons until you've answered. A wrong answer followed by the explanation sticks better than a right answer you looked up. Each explanation links to the lesson that taught it, so you know exactly what to reread.

A new 10 Gbps switch connects to a file server 80 m away over the building's existing Cat 6 cabling. Every cable tests fine for continuity, but the link keeps falling back to 1 Gbps. What's the most likely explanation?

Cat 6 does 1 Gbps to the full 100 m, but 10 Gbps only up to about 55 m. At 80 m you need Cat 6A ("augmented"), which carries 10 Gbps the whole 100 m. The plenum rating is about fire safety, not speed, and Auto-MDIX is about pin order, not distance. See lesson 0037.

An auditor flags that the office's network switches are managed with a text console over TCP port 23, and asks you to fix it. What's the right change?

Port 23 is Telnet, which sends everything, passwords included, in the clear. SSH on port 22 gives the same text console but encrypts the whole session. HTTP and FTP are just as unencrypted, and RDP is a full Windows desktop, not a switch console. See lesson 0003, 40 days ago.

A closet-to-closet uplink goes dark after its transceiver fails. A technician swaps in a spare from the drawer labeled "SM, 1310 nm". The fiber patch cable is aqua. The link still won't come up. Why?

"SM" means a single-mode optic, while an aqua jacket usually means multimode (OM3/OM4). You can't mix them: the optic and the cable must match. Fiber carries light, so it's immune to electrical interference, and multimode reaches far beyond copper's 100 m (about 2 km on the exam). Check the jacket printing, not just the color. See lesson 0039.

A user says "the internet is down." Their PC shows IP 192.168.1.57, mask 255.255.255.0 and gateway 192.168.1.1. They can open https://203.0.113.10 by its number, but no website loads by name. What's the most likely cause?

Reaching an internet address by number proves the IP, mask and gateway all work. Only names fail, so the fourth setting, DNS, is the broken one. An APIPA address would start with 169.254 and reach almost nothing; with no gateway, nothing off the local network would load at all. See lesson 0001, 42 days ago.

A user's Thunderbolt dock came without a cable, so they used the USB-C cable from their phone charger. The laptop charges through the dock, but both monitors stay black, and the keyboard plugged into the dock feels sluggish. What's the most likely cause?

USB-C is a shape, not a speed. Cheap charging cables often carry power plus USB 2.0 data (480 Mbps) and nothing else: no Thunderbolt and no video lanes. Charging works, so the power side is fine; both monitors failing at once points at the one thing they share. Use the dock's rated Thunderbolt cable (look for the bolt icon). See lesson 0040 and lesson 0041.

New cabling will run from the network closet up a vertical shaft to the next floor, then across the space above the drop ceiling where the building's air returns flow. The installer wants one cable type for the whole run. Which?

The ceiling space moves air, so it's a plenum, and it needs low-smoke CMP cable. CMP can replace CMR (riser) anywhere, but never the other way around, so CMP is the one type that's legal for both parts. Direct burial is for running in the ground; ordinary PVC gives off toxic smoke in a fire. Local fire code has the final say. See lesson 0037.

Three access points in an open-plan office all run 2.4 GHz on channels 1, 3 and 6. Users near the middle AP complain the Wi-Fi is slow and drops. What's the best channel plan?

In 2.4 GHz, channels are only 5 MHz apart but each signal is about 20 MHz wide, so neighbors overlap. Channel 3 steps on both 1 and 6. 1, 6 and 11 are the only three that don't overlap (in the US). Wider channels make it worse: fewer fit, which is why 2.4 GHz stays at 20 MHz. See lesson 0006, 37 days ago.

A technician adds a second SATA hard drive to a desktop. On boot you can hear it spin up, but it doesn't appear in the UEFI setup, Disk Management or Get-PhysicalDisk. What was most likely missed?

A spinning drive has power, so the 15-pin plug is fine. Not showing up even in UEFI means the motherboard can't see it at all: check the 7-pin SATA data cable at both ends. A driver problem wouldn't hide it from UEFI, and an unformatted drive still shows up in Disk Management. See lesson 0042.

A hot desk has an older monitor with only a DVI-D input and built-in speakers. A user connects their laptop's HDMI port with a passive HDMI-to-DVI adapter. The picture is perfect, but no sound comes from the monitor. Why?

HDMI and DVI-D share the same digital video, so a passive adapter gives a clean picture, but DVI is video only: the audio is dropped. Sound has to come from the laptop or a separate cable (or the monitor's own audio-in jack, if it has one). An active converter is only needed for digital-to-analog (VGA); dual link is about resolution; DVI-D is digital. See lesson 0041.

A user can sign in, browse the web and use Teams, but their mapped drive \\fileserver\shared times out. From their PC, Test-NetConnection fileserver -Port 445 fails, while the same test on port 443 succeeds. What's blocked?

Every \\server\share path and mapped drive uses SMB, which modern Windows runs directly on TCP 445. The test shows 445 failing while the server is otherwise reachable, so look for a firewall rule on the path or the server. LDAP (389) and LDAPS (636) are directory lookups, not file shares. See lesson 0005, 38 days ago.

A technician must connect two old industrial switches directly to each other. Neither supports Auto-MDIX, and their ports are the same kind. Which cable will make the link work?

Two devices of the same kind both send on pins 1–2 and receive on 3–6. A crossover cable (A on one end, B on the other) swaps the orange and green pairs, so one side's transmit lands on the other's receive. With Auto-MDIX the port would swap them itself and any straight-through cable would do; without it, it can't. Category and jacket don't change the pin order. See lesson 0038.

A user wants to move their old eSATA backup drive to a shelf 3 m from the PC, using a longer eSATA cable. They also ask why the drive needs its own power brick. Which statement is correct?

eSATA is shielded with stronger signal levels, so it reaches about 2 m (internal SATA about 1 m): 3 m is past the limit. It carries no power, hence the brick (eSATAp added power but never caught on). Its plug is different from internal SATA, and it runs at SATA speeds, not faster. For a new setup, a USB 3.x drive does data and power on one cable. See lesson 0042.

A new Wi-Fi 6E access point broadcasts a test network on the 6 GHz band only. Newer laptops join it fine, but one with an 802.11ac adapter doesn't even list the network. Why?

802.11ac (Wi-Fi 5) is 5 GHz only. The 6 GHz band needs Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 on both ends, so the laptop's radio can't hear that network at all. Backward compatibility only works inside a band the old adapter supports: offer the network on 5 GHz too. Channel 11 is a 2.4 GHz channel. See lesson 0007 and lesson 0006, 36 days ago.

The network team made a bad change, and a core switch can no longer be reached over the network. The switch has an RS-232 console port. Your laptop has only USB-C and Thunderbolt ports. What do you bring?

A console port speaks serial (RS-232) and works even when the network config is broken, which is exactly what it's for. Laptops have no DB-9, so a USB-to-serial adapter shows up in Windows as a COM port that a terminal program uses. Any Ethernet option still depends on the broken network settings. See lesson 0040.

Marketing starts sending newsletters through a third-party mail service, using the company's own domain in the From address. Recipients' message headers show spf=fail. What's the right fix?

SPF is the domain's allow-list of sending servers, and the new service isn't on it. Add the service's include: to the existing SPF TXT record: a domain may have only one, and a second record breaks both. Tightening DMARC would make the problem worse; MX is for receiving mail. Setting up DKIM signing for the service helps too. See lesson 0013, 30 days ago.

Explain it in your own words

Say or write the answer before you open each one. If you can explain it to a coworker, you know it.

A coworker asks why Ethernet wires are twisted, and why a desktop with one damaged pair links at 100 Mbps instead of failing completely. Explain both. (lesson 0037 and lesson 0038)

Each pair carries the same signal in opposite polarity. Noise hits both wires about equally, and the receiver looks at the difference, so the noise cancels; different twist rates per pair also cut crosstalk. Gigabit Ethernet uses all four pairs, while 100 Mbps needs only two, so with one pair broken the link falls back to the slower speed instead of dropping.

Explain single-mode vs. multimode fiber to someone choosing cable for two jobs: between two closets in one building, and between two sites 20 km apart. (lesson 0039)

Multimode has a wider core where light bounces along many paths, which smear over distance: short runs (about 2 km), cheap LED optics, usually an orange or aqua jacket. Right for closet to closet. Single-mode has a narrow core with one path: long runs (up to about 100 km), laser optics, usually yellow. Right for 20 km. Optics and cable must match.

A user says "all USB-C cables are the same, right?" Explain why not, and how video gets through a USB-C port. (lesson 0040 and lesson 0041)

USB-C is a connector shape, not a speed. The cable and port might carry only USB 2.0 (480 Mbps), USB 3.2 (5–20 Gbps), Thunderbolt (40 Gbps or more), power, or video. Video comes through DisplayPort Alternate Mode: some high-speed lanes carry DisplayPort instead of USB data. A USB 2.0-only cable has no such lanes, so no picture.

A PC shows an address starting with 169.254. Explain what that address is, where it came from, and where you'd look first. (lesson 0001, 42 days ago)

It's an APIPA (link-local) address: Windows gave it to itself because it asked for an address and no DHCP server answered. It has no gateway and no DNS, so it reaches almost nothing. Look at the path to DHCP: the cable, Wi-Fi, switch port, or the DHCP server (or a full scope), not the user's account.

In one sentence each: what do SPF, DKIM and DMARC check, and who does the checking? (lesson 0013, 30 days ago)

SPF: is the server that delivered this allowed to send for the domain (an allow-list)? DKIM: was the message signed by the domain and unchanged since (a signature checked with a public key in DNS)? DMARC: if SPF and DKIM don't pass with an aligned domain, what to do (none, quarantine, reject) and where to send reports. The receiving server checks all three.
