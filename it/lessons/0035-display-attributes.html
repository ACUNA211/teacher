How many, how close, how fast, how colorful: display attributes

About 10 minutes. Yesterday sorted displays by where the light comes from. Today covers the four numbers on every monitor's spec sheet: resolution, pixel density, refresh rate and color gamut.[1] The win: read a spec sheet, or a "my screen looks wrong" ticket, and name the attribute it's about.

Why this matters at work

"Everything looks blurry on my new monitor" and "the screen feels choppy since I docked" are common tickets after someone gets new hardware. They usually have nothing to do with the account or the device's management: the fix is a Windows display setting. Knowing the four attributes lets you check the right one in a minute instead of swapping cables.

1. Resolution: how many pixels

Resolution is the pixel count, written width × height: 1920×1080 (1080p, "Full HD"), 2560×1440 (1440p), 3840×2160 (4K UHD). An LCD or OLED panel has a fixed grid of physical pixels, its native resolution. Windows marks it (Recommended) in the resolution list.[2] Set anything else and the panel has to stretch the image across pixels that don't line up, so text looks soft or blurry.

Text too small at native resolution? Don't lower the resolution: raise Scale (125%, 150%) in the same Settings page. Scaling makes things bigger and keeps them sharp.

2. Pixel density: how close together

Pixel density is how many pixels fit in one inch of screen, in pixels per inch (PPI). Higher density looks crisper.[1] It depends on resolution and size together, which is the point the exam likes to test:

ScreenResolutionAbout
24-inch monitor1920×108092 PPI
27-inch monitor2560×1440109 PPI
14-inch laptop1920×1080157 PPI
27-inch monitor3840×2160163 PPI

Same 1080p, very different sharpness: the small laptop packs those pixels into much less space. (The math: diagonal in pixels, √(width² + height²), divided by the diagonal in inches.)

3. Refresh rate: how fast

The refresh rate is how many times per second the display redraws the picture, in hertz (Hz): 1 Hz = one refresh per second. 60 Hz is the common standard; gaming displays go up to about 240 Hz. Higher means smoother motion.[1] Two traps: Windows often starts a new monitor at 60 Hz, so a 144 Hz monitor can quietly run at 60 until someone changes it; and the cable or port must be able to carry the higher rate at that resolution.[2]

4. Color gamut: how colorful

The color gamut is the range of colors a display can show. Spec sheets give it as a percentage of a standard color space:[1][3]

Color spaceSizeUsed for
sRGBSmallest; the baselineThe web, office work, most everyday screens
Adobe RGBWider than sRGB (more greens)Photography and print
DCI-P3Wider than sRGB, about as wide as Adobe RGBVideo, cinema, HDR

"100% sRGB" is fine for a spreadsheet. A photographer preparing prints wants high Adobe RGB coverage; a video editor wants high DCI-P3 coverage.

Memory hook

How many, how close, how fast, how colorful. Resolution = how many pixels (use native). Pixel density = how close (PPI; size matters). Refresh rate = how fast (Hz; 60 standard, up to 240 gaming). Color gamut = how colorful (sRGB web, Adobe RGB print, DCI-P3 video).

Try it (3 minutes)

  1. Open Settings → System → Display. Under Display resolution, find the entry marked (Recommended): that's your panel's native resolution. Note the Scale value too.
  2. Click Advanced display. Under Choose a refresh rate, check whether you're running the highest rate your monitor offers.[2]
  3. Or ask PowerShell: 
    Get-CimInstance Win32_VideoController |
  Select-Object Name, CurrentHorizontalResolution, CurrentVerticalResolution, CurrentRefreshRate
A user's new 27-inch 1440p monitor makes text look fuzzy. Settings show 1920×1080. What do you change, and what if they then say the text is too small? (then click to check)

Set the resolution back to 2560×1440, the native one marked (Recommended); a non-native resolution gets stretched and blurs. If text is then too small, raise Scale (for example 125%) instead of lowering the resolution.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user says their new 144 Hz monitor feels no smoother than the old one. The picture is sharp and colors look right. What should you check first?

Smoothness of motion is the refresh rate. Windows often runs a new monitor at 60 Hz until someone picks a higher rate under Advanced display; the cable must also support it.

Two monitors both run at 1920×1080. One is 24 inches, the other 32 inches. Which statement is true?

Same number of pixels spread over a smaller area means more pixels per inch, so the 24-inch has the higher pixel density and looks crisper. Total pixels are the same.

A marketing team's video editor needs a monitor for color-accurate film and HDR work. Which spec matters most?

DCI-P3 is the cinema and HDR color space, so its coverage is what a video editor needs. sRGB is the small everyday baseline; Adobe RGB would suit photography for print.

A laptop screen's text looks soft and slightly smeared, but icons are the expected size. Settings show a resolution not marked (Recommended). What's the fix?

A fixed-pixel panel looks sharp only at its native resolution. Anything else is scaled across pixels that don't line up, which softens text. Use Scale, not resolution, to change size.

From memory: the four display attributes, the unit for two of them, and the three color spaces with what each is for. (then click to check)

Resolution (pixel count; use native), pixel density (PPI), refresh rate (Hz; 60 standard, up to 240 gaming), color gamut. sRGB web and everyday, Adobe RGB photography and print, DCI-P3 video and cinema.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A laptop is running (fan, Caps Lock light) but the screen is black. What 30-second test splits "backlight" from "graphics", and what does each result mean? (lesson 0034, yesterday)

The flashlight test: shine a light at an angle on the screen. A faint picture means the LCD is drawing the image but the backlight (or an old CCFL screen's inverter) has failed. No picture points at graphics, cable or panel; an external monitor narrows it down.

An employee leaves. On their personal phone (BYOD) you should remove only work data; on a company-owned phone you can erase everything. Which two actions are these? (lesson 0028, 7 days ago)

Retire (selective wipe) removes company data, apps and settings and leaves personal data alone: right for BYOD. Wipe restores the device to factory settings: for corporate-owned devices.

A user can't open a mapped drive on a file server, and a firewall rule is suspected. Which TCP port does modern SMB use, and which older protocol used 137–139? (lesson 0005, 30 days ago)

Modern SMB runs directly over TCP 445. NetBIOS (over TCP/IP) used ports 137–139, which older SMB rode on.
