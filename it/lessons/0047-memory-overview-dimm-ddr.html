Sticks of memory: DIMM, SO-DIMM and DDR

About 10 minutes. Cables and connectors are done; now we open the case. Objective 3.3 starts with RAM: the module shapes (DIMM, SO-DIMM) and the generations (DDR3, DDR4, DDR5).[1] The win: look at a stick (or a spec sheet) and say whether it fits the machine in front of you.

Why this matters at work

"My laptop is slow" tickets often end with "add RAM". Before anyone orders, you need two facts about the device: which shape (desktop DIMM or laptop SO-DIMM) and which generation (DDR4 or DDR5). Get either wrong and the stick won't even go in. Many thin laptops also have RAM soldered to the board, so there's nothing to add at all.

1. What RAM does

RAM (random access memory) is the computer's short-term workspace: the running OS, open apps and their data live there while you work. It's fast, but volatile: when the power goes off, it's empty. Storage (SSD, HDD) is the long-term shelf. Too little RAM means Windows keeps swapping data out to the much slower drive, and everything feels sluggish.[1]

2. Two shapes: DIMM and SO-DIMM

ModuleWhat it isWhere
DIMM (dual inline memory module)Full-size stick, about 13 cm long, stands upright in a slot with clips at each end.Desktops, servers
SO-DIMM (small outline DIMM)About half the length, lies flat or at an angle; side clips hold it down.Laptops, mini PCs, some all-in-ones

"Dual inline" just means the contacts on the front and back of the stick are separate signals.[2]

3. SDRAM and DDR generations

Modern RAM is SDRAM: synchronous DRAM, timed to the system clock. DDR (double data rate) SDRAM moves data on both the rising and falling edge of each clock tick, twice per cycle.[1] Each generation is faster and uses less power, and each one is keyed differently: the notch in the gold edge sits in a different place, so the wrong generation physically won't seat.[2]

GenerationDIMM pinsSO-DIMM pinsVoltage
DDR32402041.5 V (DDR3L 1.35 V)

DDR4 and DDR5 desktop sticks both have 288 pins, so the notch, not the pin count, is the real check.[2] Two DDR5 changes worth one line each: every DDR5 stick is split into two independent 32-bit subchannels, and it carries its own power regulator chip (PMIC) on the module.[3]

Rule of thumb

Match shape (DIMM vs. SO-DIMM) and generation (DDR3/4/5) to the motherboard. There is no backward compatibility between DDR generations: a DDR4 stick will not work in a DDR5 slot, ever.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. On a Windows PC, open Task Manager → Performance → Memory. Read Speed, Slots used (e.g. "2 of 2" means no free slot) and Form factor (DIMM or SODIMM).
  2. For the generation, run in PowerShell:
    Get-CimInstance Win32_PhysicalMemory | Format-Table DeviceLocator, Capacity, Speed, SMBIOSMemoryType
    SMBIOSMemoryType: 24 = DDR3, 26 = DDR4, 34 = DDR5.[4][5] (Some newer laptops with soldered memory report LPDDR codes instead.)
Task Manager says "Form factor: SODIMM, Slots used: 1 of 2", and PowerShell shows SMBIOSMemoryType 26. What do you order? (then click to check)

A DDR4 SO-DIMM: laptop-size, generation 4 (26), and there's one free slot. Ideally match the existing stick's speed and size so the pair runs in dual channel.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user wants more memory in their laptop. The short module you remove lies flat under a panel. What kind of module is it?

Laptops use the SO-DIMM (small outline DIMM), about half the length of a desktop DIMM. An M.2 SSD is also small, but it's storage, not RAM (lesson 0023).

A tech tries to install a 288-pin DDR4 DIMM into a new desktop with DDR5 slots. Both have 288 pins. What happens?

DDR generations are not compatible. DDR4 and DDR5 share a pin count, but the notch is in a different place, so the stick physically won't go in. Voltages and signaling differ too (1.2 V vs. 1.1 V).

A user's PC freezes for seconds when switching between many browser tabs, and the drive light stays busy. What's the most likely cause?

When RAM fills up, Windows moves data out to the much slower drive (the page file) and back. The busy drive and pauses are the sign. RAM is volatile, and a wrong-pin stick wouldn't fit at all.

What does "double data rate" mean in DDR SDRAM?

DDR transfers data on both the rising and the falling edge of each clock tick: twice per cycle. Pairs of sticks help with multi-channel memory, but that's a separate idea (next lesson).

From memory: pins and voltage for a DDR3, DDR4 and DDR5 desktop DIMM. (then click to check)

DDR3 240 pins, 1.5 V · DDR4 288 pins, 1.2 V · DDR5 288 pins, 1.1 V. Same pins for 4 and 5, different notch.

Adapter vs. converter: what's the difference, and which one does DVI-D to VGA need? (lesson 0044, 3 days ago)

An adapter is passive: it only rewires pins between two connectors that carry the same signal. A converter is active and changes the signal. DVI-D is digital only and VGA is analog, so it needs a converter.

A USB-C laptop port shows a lightning-bolt icon. What can it do that a plain USB-C port may not? (lesson 0040, 7 days ago)

It's Thunderbolt: up to 40 Gbps (Thunderbolt 3/4) over the USB-C connector, with displays and docks on one cable. USB-C is only the plug shape; what the port carries depends on the standard behind it.

Black Friday traffic triples, and the cloud adds servers on its own, then removes them next week. Which cloud characteristic is that? (lesson 0033, 14 days ago)

Rapid elasticity: capacity grows and shrinks with demand, usually without users noticing. Paired with metered billing, you pay only for what you used.
