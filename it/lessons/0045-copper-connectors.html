Name that plug: copper connectors

About 10 minutes. You've met the cables; now the ends. The A+ exam shows you a connector and expects a name and a job.[1] Four are new today (RJ11, F connector, punchdown block, Molex) and four you already know (USB, USB-C, Lightning, DB-9). The win: walk up to a wall plate, a modem or the inside of a PC and name every copper plug you see.

Why this matters at work

"The network is down" sometimes means the laptop's Ethernet cable is in the phone jack. An RJ11 plug can sit in an RJ45 port and an RJ45 plug looks almost right next to a phone jack, so a quick look at the plug saves a long remote session.[2]

1. The new four

ConnectorLooks likeUsed for
RJ11Small clip plug, 6 positions, usually 2 or 4 wires (6P2C / 6P4C)Telephone lines and DSL[1][2]
RJ45Wider clip plug, 8 positions, 8 wires (8P8C)Ethernet on twisted pair (lesson 0038)
F connectorThreaded round coax plug with a center wireCable TV and the cable modem (lesson 0021), usually on RG-6 coax[1][3]
Punchdown blockA row of slots in the wiring closet; each wire is pressed in with a punchdown tool (lesson 0019)Terminating the permanent cable runs from desks. 110 blocks are the common data type; 66 blocks are older, for phones.
MolexChunky 4-pin internal power plug, no latchOlder drives and fans. Yellow = 12 V, red = 5 V, two black grounds. No 3.3 V; SATA power has largely replaced it (lesson 0042).[4]

Molex is the company that made the connector; the name stuck to the plug.[1]

2. The familiar four (quick recap)

  • USB (Type-A, Type-B, Micro, Mini): the rectangular host plug and its smaller device-side cousins (lesson 0040).
  • USB-C: 24 pins, reversible; also carries Thunderbolt and DisplayPort video (lesson 0025, lesson 0041).
  • Lightning: Apple's 8-pin reversible plug on older iPhones; newer iPhones moved to USB-C (lesson 0025).
  • DB-9: the 9-pin D-shaped serial (RS-232) plug, still found on switch and router console ports (lesson 0040).
Memory hook

RJ11 = 1 phone line, small. RJ45 = Ethernet, wide. F screws on (coax, cable modem). Punchdown = closet, no plug at all. Molex = yellow 12, red 5.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. Look behind your desk, your home router or a PC. For each copper plug, say its name and what it carries: RJ45 (Ethernet), RJ11 (phone/DSL), F (coax from the provider), USB-C, and so on.
  2. If you have a cable modem, find the F connector: it's the one marked Cable or RF In. Hand-tight is enough; it screws on so it can't be tugged loose.[3]
Someone shows you a plug with eight gold contacts and a clip, about the width of a finger. What is it, and where else could you see eight contacts? (then click to check)

An RJ45 (8P8C) Ethernet plug. RJ11 has the same clip shape but is narrower, with 6 positions and usually only 2 or 4 contacts wired. (Lightning also has 8 contacts, but it's a small flat reversible tongue, not a clip plug.)

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A home user with DSL service says the internet stopped after moving furniture. The modem's power and Ethernet look fine. Which cable should you check next?

DSL runs over phone lines, so the modem connects to the wall with an RJ11 cord. The F connector is for cable (coax) service, not DSL.

A technician is connecting a cable modem to the provider's line. Which connector screws onto the modem's input?

Cable internet arrives on coax, and coax ends in a threaded F connector. RJ45 is on the other side of the modem, toward your network.

An old optical drive in a desktop has a 4-pin power socket with no latch. Which voltages does that plug deliver?

That's a Molex peripheral connector: yellow 12 V, red 5 V, two black grounds. It has no 3.3 V, one reason SATA power replaced it.

New desk jacks are being installed. In the wiring closet, the other end of each cable run is pressed into a row of slots with a tool. What is that row called?

A punchdown block terminates permanent runs without a plug: each wire is punched into a slot. 110 blocks are the usual type for data; 66 blocks are older, for phones. Often it's built into the back of a patch panel (lesson 0020).

From memory: name the eight copper connectors from today and one job for each. (then click to check)

RJ11 phone/DSL · RJ45 Ethernet · F coax, cable modem · punchdown block closet termination · Molex internal 12/5 V power · USB peripherals · USB-C reversible data, power and video · Lightning older iPhones · DB-9 serial console.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

How long can an internal SATA cable be, and how long an eSATA cable? Can you swap them? (lesson 0042, 3 days ago)

Internal SATA: up to 1 m. eSATA: about 2 m (stronger signal, shielded). They are not interchangeable: the plugs are shaped differently.

What's the only difference between T568A and T568B, and when do you get a crossover cable? (lesson 0038, 7 days ago)

The orange and green pairs swap places. Same standard on both ends = straight-through; A on one end and B on the other = crossover. Auto-MDIX on modern ports makes crossovers rarely needed.

Type 1 vs. type 2 hypervisor: which runs on bare metal, and which kind is VirtualBox on a laptop? (lesson 0031, 14 days ago)

Type 1 (native / bare metal) runs directly on the hardware, e.g. Hyper-V, ESXi. Type 2 (hosted) runs as an app on top of an OS, like VirtualBox on a laptop.
