How to take it

No scrolling back to the lessons until you've answered. A wrong answer followed by the explanation sticks better than a right answer you looked up. Each explanation links to the lesson that taught it, so you know exactly what to reread.

A manager forwards a quote for a new file server's memory: "32 GB DDR5-4800 DIMM, 64-bit, on-die ECC." The server vendor requires ECC memory. Is this the right part?

Every DDR5 chip has on-die ECC, but it only fixes errors inside each chip, not on the way to the CPU. An ECC module is 72 bits wide (64 data + 8 check bits), and this quote says 64-bit: ordinary memory. Parity would be worse, not better: it detects an error but can't fix it, and the system usually halts. Order a part whose spec line says ECC as a module feature. See lesson 0048.

A small office's VoIP phones sound choppy during busy hours. A coworker suggests reconfiguring the phones to send voice over TCP "so no audio gets lost." What's wrong with that idea?

Live voice rides UDP on purpose. A lost packet becomes a tiny glitch and the call moves on. TCP would stop and re-send, so audio arrives late, which is worse in a conversation than a missing syllable. TCP is the connection-oriented one, and both protocols use ports. The real fix for choppy calls on a busy network is more bandwidth or traffic priority (QoS), not TCP. See lesson 0002, 48 days ago.

A conference-room PC has a DHCP reservation so the room's booking display can always find it. IT replaces the PC's broken USB-to-Ethernet adapter, and now the PC gets a random address from the pool. Why?

A USB-to-Ethernet adapter is its own network card with its own MAC address. A DHCP reservation ties one IP to one MAC, so it followed the old adapter, not the PC. Update the reservation with the new adapter's MAC (Get-NetAdapter shows it). An exclusion does the opposite: DHCP would never hand that address out. See lesson 0044 and lesson 0014, 36 days ago.

A video editor wants the fastest possible scratch drive in their desktop. It has a free SATA port and a free PCIe x4 slot that takes an NVMe SSD card. Which do you recommend, and why?

Any SSD on SATA tops out near 600 MB/s, held back by AHCI, a protocol built for spinning disks with one queue of 32 commands. NVMe over PCIe was designed for flash, with thousands of deep queues, and reaches several GB/s. A 15,000 rpm drive is still a spinning disk, and an SSHD only caches the files you use most. See lesson 0049.

A warehouse wants pallets logged automatically as forklifts carry them through the dock door, a few meters from the reader. The tags must be cheap and have no battery. Which technology fits?

A passive RFID tag has no battery: the reader's radio signal powers it just long enough to reply with its ID, with no tap and no pairing. NFC works at a few centimeters and needs someone to tap; Bluetooth beacons need a battery; fixed wireless links two buildings, not pallets. See lesson 0009, 41 days ago.

A user moved desks and now sees "Network cable unplugged." The cord from the PC to the wall jack is pushed in at both ends, but its plugs are narrower than the ports and show only four gold contacts. What's going on?

A narrow clip plug with 6 positions and 2 or 4 contacts is RJ11, a phone/DSL cord. It can sit in an RJ45 port, but it doesn't reach the pins Ethernet uses, so there's no link. Ethernet uses the wider 8-contact RJ45 (8P8C). A crossover cable still has RJ45 plugs, the jacket rating is about fire safety, and an F connector screws onto coax. See lesson 0045.

The provider replaced a fiber customer's ONT. The technician grabs a spare patch cable with blue SC plugs and connects it to the new ONT's green SC port. The link comes up but shows high signal loss. What's the most likely cause?

Connector body colors code the polish: blue = UPC (flat tip), green = APC (angled 8°). A flat face against an angled one leaves a gap: light is lost, and the tips can be damaged. Use a green APC cable. SC is push-pull (ST is the one that twists), a swapped duplex pair gives no link at all rather than a weak one, and beige marks multimode, not a fix. See lesson 0046.

A company moved its website to a new host and changed the A record 20 minutes ago. Some users already see the new site; others still get the old one. The record's TTL is 3600. What's going on?

The TTL (time to live, in seconds) says how long clients and other DNS servers may cache an answer. 3600 s is an hour, so anyone who looked the name up just before the change keeps the old address for up to an hour. Wait it out (or ipconfig /flushdns on one PC); next time, lower the TTL a day before a move. MX is only for mail, and AAAA is IPv6. See lesson 0012, 38 days ago.

A user's laptop has 8 GB of RAM and needs 16 GB. Task Manager shows Form factor: SODIMM and Slots used: 2 of 2, and PowerShell reports SMBIOSMemoryType 34 for both 4 GB sticks. What do you order?

Three facts decide it. SODIMM: laptop-size modules, not desktop DIMMs. Type 34 = DDR5 (26 would be DDR4), and generations never mix. 2 of 2: no free slot, so both sticks get replaced. A matched pair keeps it in dual channel. See lesson 0047 and lesson 0048.

A branch office's internet slows to a crawl during heavy rainstorms and recovers when the weather clears. Even on sunny days, every remote-desktop click lags about half a second. What connection does the branch most likely have?

Two clues, one answer. Rain fade is satellite's weather weakness, and a constant half-second lag is the round trip to a geostationary satellite about 36,000 km up. A blocked line of sight (WISP) doesn't come and go with rain, DSL distance doesn't change with the weather, and shared cable slows at busy times of day. See lesson 0018, 32 days ago.

A desktop with integrated graphics has a single 8 GB DDR4 stick, and its user says video playback and screen sharing stutter. Which inexpensive upgrade will help the graphics most?

Integrated graphics use system RAM, so memory bandwidth is graphics bandwidth. One stick uses one channel; a matching stick in the paired (often color-coded) slot gives dual channel, roughly doubling bandwidth. One bigger stick is still single channel, ECC adds no speed (and needs board support), and a hard drive for swap is slower than any RAM. See lesson 0048.

A conference room's projector has only a VGA input. A visitor's laptop has only USB-C ports. Which of these will get a picture on the screen?

Video over USB-C is DisplayPort Alt Mode: digital. VGA is analog. Different signals need an active converter with a chip that changes the signal. The HDMI adapter's output doesn't fit a VGA input, the laptop has no DVI-I port, and a USB-A adapter carries data, not video. See lesson 0044 and lesson 0041.

A user's account shows a sign-in at 3 a.m. from another country. Before you reset the password, you want every sign-in to that account last week, with times and locations. Which part of AAA gives you that?

Accounting is the record of what happened: who signed in, when, from where, to what. In Microsoft Entra ID that's the sign-in logs. Authentication is proving who you are (password, MFA); authorization is what you're then allowed to do (group and app assignments). Neither keeps the history. See lesson 0010, 40 days ago.

A small office needs about 40 TB of storage for nightly backups. The budget is tight, and how fast a restore runs matters little. Which drives fit best?

Hard drives give the most capacity for the least money, which is exactly what bulk backups need when speed doesn't matter. SSDs win on speed, silence and surviving drops, but cost more per terabyte. An SSHD's small flash cache only speeds up files used often, and backups are written once and rarely read. See lesson 0049.

During a company-wide video town hall streamed from a cloud service, remote staff on VPN say the stream keeps buffering, and the HQ internet link shows 100% use. The VPN profile sends all traffic through HQ. What change would relieve the link most?

With a full tunnel, every remote viewer's stream comes in through HQ's internet link and goes back out through the VPN: twice over one link. A split tunnel sends only company traffic through the VPN, so cloud video goes straight from the provider to each home connection. The trade-off: HQ can no longer inspect that traffic. A VLAN divides a local network, and site-to-site joins offices, not single users. See lesson 0016, 34 days ago.

Explain it in your own words

Say or write the answer before you open each one. If you can explain it to a coworker, you know it.

A coworker asks: "ECC, parity, non-parity: what's the difference, and doesn't every DDR5 stick have ECC anyway?" Explain. (lesson 0048)

Non-parity: no checking; a flipped bit goes unnoticed. Parity: one check bit per byte; it can detect a single-bit error but not fix it, so the system usually halts. ECC: extra check bits (72 bits wide instead of 64) that detect and correct single-bit errors so the system keeps running; it needs board and CPU support. DDR5's on-die ECC only protects inside each chip, so a DDR5 stick isn't an ECC module unless its spec says so.

Explain the difference between an adapter and a converter, and give one video example of each. (lesson 0044)

An adapter is passive: it only rewires one connector shape to another, so both sides must already carry the same signal (DVI to HDMI, DVI-I to VGA). A converter is active: a chip changes the signal, often with its own power (DVI-D, HDMI, DisplayPort or USB-C to VGA, digital to analog). The test is never "does it fit?" but "is the same signal on both sides?"

A user bought "a fast SSD" and asks why their friend's SSD benchmarks five times faster. Explain what else decides an SSD's speed. (lesson 0049)

How it connects. A SATA SSD uses AHCI, a protocol built for spinning disks (one queue of 32 commands), and is capped near 600 MB/s. An NVMe SSD talks straight over PCIe lanes with thousands of deep queues and reaches several GB/s. "SSD" says what the drive is; the bus says how fast it can go. Get-PhysicalDisk shows both: MediaType and BusType.

For ST, SC and LC: how does each lock, and where would you expect to find it? (lesson 0046)

ST: round, bayonet (push and twist), older multimode panels. SC: square, push-pull, provider fiber and ONTs. LC: small, latch clip, usually in duplex pairs, in the SFP modules of today's switches. Memory hook: Stick and Twist, Stick and Click, Little Connector.

Explain a DHCP exclusion vs. a DHCP reservation, and when you'd use each. (lesson 0014, 36 days ago)

An exclusion is a set of addresses DHCP never hands out: use it for devices with hand-typed static addresses (router, some servers), so DHCP doesn't give their IP to someone else. A reservation always gives one IP to one MAC address: use it for devices that should keep the same address but still get their settings from DHCP, like printers. Because it's tied to the MAC, replacing the network adapter breaks it.
