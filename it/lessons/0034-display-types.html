Where the light comes from: LCD, OLED, Mini-LED and touchscreens

About 10 minutes. Virtualization and cloud are done, so today starts the biggest exam domain: Hardware (25%). First video: display types. Messer covers LCD technologies, OLED, Mini-LED, touchscreens, digitizers and inverters.[1] One question sorts almost all of it: where does the light come from? The win: hear a symptom or a requirement and name the display part involved.

Why this matters at work

"My laptop screen is black" is a classic ticket. Knowing that an LCD is a light source plus a picture layer gives you a 30-second test that decides between a remote fix (graphics driver, display settings) and a hardware repair (backlight). The same split, picture vs. touch layer, tells you whether a tablet with dead taps needs a new screen or just a reboot.

LCD: a picture that needs a light behind it

Liquid crystals don't make light. An LCD shines a backlight through the crystals and color filters, and the crystals twist to let more or less light through each pixel.[1][2] Three panel types twist the crystals differently:

PanelStrengthWeaknessPick it for
TN (twisted nematic)Fastest response, cheapest; the oldest designColors shift when viewed off-center; weaker colorCompetitive gaming, budget monitors[2]
IPS (in-plane switching)Best color accuracy, widest viewing anglesCosts more than TNPhoto and graphic design, shared screens[2]
VA (vertical alignment)Best contrast of the three (deep blacks)Slower response than TNA good middle ground; movies[2]

The backlight, and the inverter

Older LCDs were lit by a CCFL (cold cathode fluorescent lamp), a thin tube that needs high-voltage AC. A laptop runs on DC, so a small board called the inverter converts DC to AC for the lamp.[1][4] Modern LCDs use an LED backlight instead, which runs on DC and needs no inverter. A screen sold as an "LED display" is almost always an LCD with LED backlighting, not a new kind of panel.[2]

Mini-LED is still an LCD, but the backlight is thousands of tiny LEDs grouped into local dimming zones. Zones behind dark parts of the picture turn down, so blacks get deeper and highlights brighter. Zones are bigger than pixels, so a bright object on black can show a faint halo ("blooming").[1][3]

OLED: every pixel is its own light

In an OLED (organic light-emitting diode) display, each pixel glows by itself. There's no backlight, so a black pixel is simply off: perfect blacks, thin and light panels.[1][2] The catch: the organic material wears with use, so an image left on screen for a very long time (a taskbar, a logo) can leave a permanent ghost, called burn-in. Mini-LED uses inorganic LEDs and doesn't burn in.[3]

Touchscreens and digitizers

A touchscreen is two layers doing two jobs. The display shows the picture; the digitizer is a layer that senses touch (or an active pen, as in lesson 0026) and converts it into screen coordinates.[1] So "the picture is fine but taps don't work" points at the digitizer; "taps work but the picture is broken" points at the display. On many phones and tablets the two are fused to the glass and replaced as one part.

Memory hook

Ask where the light comes from. Behind the crystals → LCD (CCFL needs an inverter; LED doesn't; Mini-LED = many dimming zones). From each pixel → OLED (perfect black, can burn in). TN is fast, IPS is true color, VA is deep black. Picture layer ≠ touch layer (digitizer).

Try it (3 minutes)

  1. The flashlight test. Next time a laptop screen looks black but the PC is clearly running (fan, power light, Caps Lock toggles), dim the room and shine a flashlight at an angle onto the screen. A faint picture means the LCD is drawing the image but nothing is lighting it: backlight (or, on old CCFL laptops, the inverter). No picture at all points toward the graphics, the cable or the panel itself; connecting an external monitor narrows it further.[4]
  2. Do I have a digitizer? On your Windows PC open Settings → System → About and find the Pen and touch line. It says whether the screen has touch or pen support, or "No pen or touch input is available for this display."
A user's laptop shows a very faint picture under a flashlight, and an external monitor works perfectly. Remote fix or hardware repair, and which part? (then click to check)

Hardware repair: the backlight (or its inverter on an older CCFL screen). The graphics are fine, since the external monitor works and the panel is drawing the image; only the light behind it is missing. No driver update will fix that.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A graphic designer needs an LCD monitor with the most accurate color, and clients often look at it from the side of the desk. Which panel type fits best?

IPS gives the best color accuracy and the widest viewing angles. TN shifts color off-center, VA is strongest at contrast, and Mini-LED is a backlight, not a panel type.

An older laptop with a CCFL-lit screen boots normally, but the display is nearly black. With a flashlight you can just make out the desktop. What is the most likely cause?

A faint image means the LCD is drawing the picture but nothing lights it. On a CCFL screen that's the lamp or the inverter that powers it. A graphics fault would leave no image to see at all.

After a drop, a tablet's picture looks perfect, but taps along the bottom edge do nothing. Which part has most likely failed?

The picture is fine, so the display side works. The digitizer is the layer that senses touch, so dead taps in one area point at it.

A lobby sign will show the same logo and menu bar 12 hours a day for years. The buyer wants deep contrast but no risk of burn-in. Which display fits best?

Mini-LED gives deep contrast through local dimming zones, and its inorganic LEDs don't burn in. OLED has the best blacks, but static images for years are exactly what causes burn-in.

From memory: the three LCD panel types and their strengths, what an inverter does, and how OLED and Mini-LED differ. (then click to check)

TN fast and cheap, IPS best color and angles, VA best contrast. The inverter turns DC into the high-voltage AC a CCFL backlight needs (LED backlights don't need one). OLED: each pixel makes its own light, no backlight, can burn in. Mini-LED: an LCD whose backlight is many small LEDs in local dimming zones, no burn-in.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A company uploads all its archives to the cloud for free, then gets a big bill the month it downloads them again. Name the two terms, and which one usually costs money. (lesson 0033, yesterday)

Ingress (data in) is usually free; egress (data out) is usually charged. Both belong to metered utilization: you pay for what you use.

Hyper-V on a server and VirtualBox on a laptop: which is type 1 and which is type 2, and what's the difference? (lesson 0031, 3 days ago)

Hyper-V is type 1 (bare metal: the hypervisor runs directly on the hardware). VirtualBox is type 2 (hosted: it's an app on top of a normal OS). Type 1 is leaner and used in data centers; type 2 is convenient on a desktop.

Two PCs on the same switch talk fine, but neither reaches the internet. Which device decides by MAC address, which by IP address, and which one do you suspect? (lesson 0020, 14 days ago)

A switch forwards by MAC address inside the local network; a router forwards by IP address between networks. Local traffic works, so suspect the router (or the PCs' default gateway setting that points at it).
