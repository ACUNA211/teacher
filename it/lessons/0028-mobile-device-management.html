Whose phone, whose data: mobile device management

About 10 minutes. An organization with many phones needs a mobile device manager (MDM): one console that sets policy on every device, whether the company owns it or the employee does.[1] The win today: match the ownership model to the right amount of control, and know which button removes only the company's data.

Why this matters at work

This is Intune's home turf. Most "I can't get email on my phone" tickets come down to one question: is this phone enrolled, or only its apps protected? The answer decides what IT can see, what a policy can block, and what happens when someone leaves. Users worry IT can read their photos; knowing the ownership model lets you answer that honestly.

What an MDM does

From one console, an administrator can:[1]

  • Allow or block apps, and push the business apps people need.
  • Turn features off, such as the camera or GPS, where policy requires it.
  • Require security settings: a screen lock, encryption, a minimum OS version.
  • Partition a personal phone: a separate work area for company apps and data, with the rest of the phone left private.
  • Remove company data remotely when a device is lost or its owner leaves.

The exam names BYOD and COPE. Intune's Android enrollment options map one-to-one onto all four rows: personally owned work profile, corporate-owned work profile, fully managed, dedicated.[3]

Two levels: manage the device, or only the apps

MDM enrolls the whole device. MAM (mobile application management) skips enrollment and protects company data inside work apps: require a PIN for the app, block copy-paste and "save as" to personal apps and storage, and wipe only the company data from the app.[4] MAM is the lightest-touch option for BYOD. The trade-off: without enrollment, IT can't push apps, certificates, or company Wi-Fi and VPN settings to the phone.[4]

Removing data: retire vs. wipe

ActionWhat it removesUse for

Source: [5]. A full wipe isn't even offered for some personal-device enrollments, by design.

Data sync and business apps

Phones sync constantly: mail, contacts and calendar from the work account, and photos and files to the owner's cloud backup. The MDM challenge is keeping those two streams apart, so company files don't end up in a personal cloud, and business apps arrive configured (the right account, the right settings) rather than set up by hand on every phone.[1]

Memory hook

BYOD = their phone, your work area. COPE = your phone, their personal use. MDM manages the device; MAM manages only the apps. Retire = take the company's data back. Wipe = take everything.

Try it (2 minutes)

See what's managing your own devices:

  • Windows: Settings → Accounts → Access work or school. A connected work account with an Info button means the PC is enrolled in an MDM.
  • iPhone: Settings → General → VPN & Device Management. Any management profile is listed there, with what it can do.
  • Android: a work profile shows up as a separate Work tab in the app drawer, with briefcase badges on work apps.

If nothing is listed but your work email app still asks for an app PIN, that's app protection (MAM) without enrollment.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user wants work email on their personal phone but refuses to enroll it: they don't want IT able to see or erase personal content. What fits best?

App protection (MAM without enrollment) protects company data inside the work apps only: an app PIN, no copy-paste or save to personal apps, and a wipe that touches only company data. The device isn't enrolled, so IT has no device-level control over personal content. Fully managed mode is for company-owned phones.

An employee leaves. Their personal phone has a work profile. You must remove company email and files but leave their photos alone. Which action?

Retire (a selective wipe) removes company apps, data and settings and leaves personal data intact. Wipe is a factory reset, wrong for a personal phone. Deleting the account blocks new sign-ins but doesn't remove data already on the phone, and the SIM has nothing to do with company data.

A company buys phones for its sales team. IT wants full control (encryption, updates, configuration) but staff may also install personal apps. Which model is this?

COPE: the company owns and fully manages the phone but lets the employee use it personally too. BYOD would mean the employee owns it; dedicated devices have no single user; app protection without enrollment gives the least control, not full control.

On a personal phone, a user tries to save a work attachment from the managed email app to their personal cloud storage, and the app refuses. Why?

App protection policies can block "save as" to unmanaged locations and copy-paste into personal apps, so company data can't leak into personal sync and backup. It's working as designed; the fix is to save to the approved work storage instead.

From memory: the difference between BYOD and COPE, between MDM and MAM, and between retire and wipe. (then click to check)

BYOD: employee owns it; IT controls only the work area. COPE: company owns and fully manages it; personal use allowed. MDM: enrolls and manages the whole device. MAM: protects data inside work apps, no enrollment. Retire: removes company data only. Wipe: factory reset.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A user switches carriers and gets a new SIM. Which identifier stays the same, the IMEI or the ICCID, and why? (lesson 0027, 1 day ago)

The IMEI stays: it identifies the phone hardware. The ICCID identifies the SIM (or eSIM profile), so it changes with the new SIM.

A new access point needs PoE but the closet switch doesn't supply power. What can you add, and what's it called in endspan/midspan terms? (lesson 0021, 7 days ago)

A PoE injector between the switch and the AP. That's midspan; power from the switch port itself is endspan.

With an 8-day DHCP lease, when does a client first try to renew, and what's the difference between a reservation and an exclusion? (lesson 0014, 14 days ago)

At T1 = half the lease (day 4), with the server that gave the lease. A reservation always gives one device (by MAC) the same address; an exclusion keeps addresses out of the pool so DHCP never hands them out.
