NFC, cameras and microphones: the laptop's senses

About 10 minutes. Yesterday covered the parts you swap: battery, keyboard, drive. Today finishes the laptop hardware video with the parts that sense the world: the NFC reader, the webcam and the microphones.[1] The win: a fixed order of checks for the most common ticket they cause, "my camera (or mic) doesn't work in my meeting".

Why this matters at work

On managed laptops, the camera can be switched off by policy, not just by the user. Intune can set the Windows Allow Camera setting to Not allowed, which stops every app from using the camera while the hardware stays in place.[4] When the Windows camera switches are greyed out with "Some settings are managed by your organization", the fix isn't on the laptop: check which configuration policy the device gets.

NFC: tap, don't wave

You met near-field communication in lesson 0009: 13.56 MHz, a few centimeters of range. Some laptops build in an NFC reader, usually under a marked spot on the palm rest.[1] Typical uses:

  • Authentication: tap a badge, phone or security key to sign in. Microsoft's passwordless security keys (FIDO2) can connect over USB, Bluetooth or NFC.[5]
  • Pairing: tap a phone or headset to start a Bluetooth pairing without typing codes.
  • Payments: the same "tap" idea as a phone at a store terminal.[1]

Range is about 4 cm, so "it doesn't read" usually means the card or phone wasn't held flat on the mark long enough.

Cameras and microphones

The webcam sits in the bezel above the screen; one or more microphones sit beside it or along the edges.[1] Three privacy features to recognize:

  • Privacy shutter: a small slider that physically covers the lens. Closed, the camera still "works", but every app shows a black image.[2]
  • Function keys: many laptops have a key (often with a slashed camera or microphone icon) that switches the camera or mic off in hardware.
  • Activity light: a small LED next to the lens that turns on while the camera is in use.

One more: Windows Hello face sign-in needs an infrared (IR) camera. A normal webcam works fine in meetings but can't do Hello face; if Facial recognition isn't offered in Sign-in options, the laptop has no IR camera.[6]

"My camera doesn't work": check in this order

Go from the physical to the managed, cheapest check first:

#LayerWhat to check
1PhysicalShutter open? Camera/mic function key on? (Black image = shutter.)
2Windows privacySettings → Privacy & security → Camera (or Microphone): Camera access on, the app allowed. Desktop programs have their own switch: Let desktop apps access your camera.[3]
3DeviceDevice Manager or Get-PnpDevice: is the camera present and OK, not Error (disabled or driver problem)?[7]
4PolicySwitches greyed out? A management policy (such as Intune's Allow Camera) is blocking it.[4]

Also ask: does it work in the Windows Camera app? If yes, the problem is the meeting app's own device setting (it picked the wrong camera or mic), not the laptop.

Memory hook

NFC: tap within 4 cm, for sign-in, pairing, paying. Camera and mic: shutter → privacy → device → policy. Hello face needs infrared.

Try it (2 minutes)

List the cameras Windows sees. In PowerShell:[7]

Get-PnpDevice -PresentOnly -Class Camera,Image

Example (yours will differ):

Status  Class   FriendlyName
------  -----   ------------
OK      Camera  Integrated Camera
OK      Camera  Integrated IR Camera

Two cameras, one of them IR: this laptop can do Windows Hello face. Status OK means the device and driver are fine (newer drivers use the Camera class, older ones Image). Then open Settings → Privacy & security → Camera and look at which apps have used it recently.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user says their camera shows only a black picture in every app. The activity light comes on, and Device Manager shows the camera working. What's the likely cause?

A black image with the camera clearly running (light on, device OK) means the lens is covered. A missing driver would make the camera disappear or show an error, not show black. NFC and the battery have nothing to do with the picture.

A laptop's microphone works in the built-in Voice Recorder app but not in a desktop conferencing program installed from an .exe. What should you check first?

The mic hardware and driver are fine: another app records. Windows has a separate privacy switch for traditional desktop programs ("Let desktop apps access your microphone"), so check that layer before touching hardware or drivers.

A laptop has an NFC reader for badge sign-in. A user holds their badge a hand's width above the palm rest and nothing happens. What do you tell them?

NFC works within about 4 cm, so a hand's width is too far: the badge has to touch (or nearly touch) the marked reader. NFC doesn't need Bluetooth pairing or Wi-Fi, and BitLocker has nothing to do with it.

On a company laptop, the camera works in no app, Device Manager shows it OK, and the camera switches in Windows Settings are greyed out as managed by the organization. What's the cause?

Greyed-out settings "managed by your organization" point to device management: for example an Intune policy with Allow Camera set to Not allowed. The hardware is fine (device OK). A shutter would show black, not block apps. Infrared only matters for Windows Hello face sign-in.

From memory: say the four layers to check, in order, when a camera or mic doesn't work. Then: what kind of camera does Windows Hello face sign-in need? (then click to check)

Physical (shutter, function key) → Windows privacy settings (including the desktop-apps switch) → device (Device Manager / Get-PnpDevice, status OK) → policy (greyed out = managed). Hello face needs an infrared (IR) camera.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A new ceiling access point needs power, but the closet switch has no PoE. What small device fixes it, and is that an endspan or a midspan? (lesson 0021, 3 days ago)

A PoE injector, placed between the normal switch port and the access point. That's a midspan; a PoE switch powering its own ports is an endspan.

Servers need storage that behaves like a local disk, on its own fast network. Is that a SAN or a NAS, and what's the difference? (lesson 0017, 7 days ago)

A SAN: block-level storage the server treats like its own disk, on an isolated high-speed network. A NAS shares files over the normal network (SMB or NFS).

Name the three A's of AAA in order, and where you'd find the third one for cloud sign-ins. (lesson 0010, 14 days ago)

Authentication (who are you?), authorization (what may you use?), accounting (the record of what happened). In Microsoft Entra ID, accounting is the sign-in logs.
