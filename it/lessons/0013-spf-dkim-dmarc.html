SPF, DKIM and DMARC: proving mail is really yours

About 10 minutes. Email was built with no proof of who sent it: anyone can type any address in the From line. Yesterday (lesson 0012) you saw that TXT records hold notes for other services. Today: the three notes that let a receiving mail server check whether a message really came from your domain.[1] This finishes the DNS Configuration video.

Why this matters at work

"My emails to a customer land in their junk folder" or "someone is sending phishing that looks like it's from us" are both questions about these three records. In Microsoft 365 you don't set SPF in an admin portal at all: it's a TXT record at your DNS host.[2] Reading a message header and seeing spf=fail or dmarc=fail tells you which record to fix.

Three records, three questions

All three live in DNS as text, and all three are checked by the receiving server, not the sender. Examples use example.com and documentation addresses.

RecordQuestion it answersWhere it lives / example
SPF
Sender Policy Framework		Is this sending server allowed to send mail for the domain?TXT at example.com
v=spf1 ip4:192.0.2.25 include:spf.protection.outlook.com -all
DKIM
DomainKeys Identified Mail		Was the message signed by the domain, and is it unchanged since?Public key at selector1._domainkey.example.com
v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIB…
DMARC
Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance		If SPF and DKIM fail, what should the receiver do, and who gets the reports?TXT at _dmarc.example.com
v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com

How each one works

  • SPF is an allow-list of senders. The receiver looks at the IP address that delivered the message and checks it against the domain's SPF list. The ending says what to do with anyone not on it: -all means hard fail, ~all means soft fail (suspicious, but deliver). A domain may have only one SPF record; adding a second one breaks both. Microsoft 365 domains need include:spf.protection.outlook.com, and Microsoft recommends -all.[2]
  • DKIM is a digital signature. The sending server signs each message with a private key and adds a DKIM-Signature header. The receiver fetches the matching public key from DNS (the s= selector in the header tells it which name to look up) and checks the signature. If someone changed the message on the way, the check fails.[3] In Microsoft 365, you publish two CNAME records (selector1._domainkey and selector2._domainkey) that point at keys Microsoft hosts, then switch signing on.[3]
  • DMARC is the policy on top. It passes when SPF or DKIM passes and the domain they checked matches the domain in the visible From address (called alignment). The p= tag says what to do with mail that fails: none (just report), quarantine (accept, but mark it, e.g. junk folder), or reject (refuse it). rua= is where daily summary reports go. Microsoft's advice: start at p=none, read the reports to find legitimate senders you forgot, then move to quarantine and finally reject.[4]
Memory hooks

SPF = the guest list (which servers may send). DKIM = the wax seal (signed, and not opened on the way). DMARC = the bouncer's instructions (what to do when the list or seal doesn't check out, and who to tell). Order to set them up: SPF, then DKIM, then DMARC.

Try it (3 minutes)

Read a real domain's records in PowerShell:[5]

Resolve-DnsName microsoft.com -Type TXT | Where-Object Strings -match 'v=spf1'
Resolve-DnsName _dmarc.microsoft.com -Type TXT

In the SPF answer, find the ending (-all or ~all). In the DMARC answer, find p=: is it none, quarantine or reject?

Then look at the receiving side. In Outlook on the web, open any email from an outside sender, choose … → View → View message details, and search for Authentication-Results. You'll see a line like spf=pass … dkim=pass … dmarc=pass. That one line is all three of today's records, checked.

Checking your own organization's domain is a good exercise, but keep the results off any public page.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A company starts sending invoices through a new online billing service. Customers report the invoices land in junk, and headers show spf=fail. What's the fix?

SPF lists the servers allowed to send for the domain, and the new service isn't on it. Edit the one existing SPF record (usually an extra include:). A second SPF record breaks SPF entirely, MX only controls incoming mail, and reject would make things worse.

A receiving server checks a message's signature against a public key it found in DNS, and confirms nothing changed in transit. Which record made that possible?

DKIM is the only one that uses a key pair: the sender signs with a private key, and the public key sits in DNS under selector._domainkey. SPF checks the sending IP, not the content.

An admin is publishing DMARC for the first time and isn't sure every legitimate sender is covered yet. Which policy should they start with?

p=none changes nothing about delivery but turns on reports (rua=), which show every server sending as your domain. Fix the forgotten senders, then step up to quarantine and reject.

Phishing mail shows your company's address in the From line. Your SPF and DKIM are set up, but receivers still deliver it to inboxes. What's most likely missing?

SPF and DKIM only report pass or fail; DMARC tells the receiver what to do with a failure and checks that the From domain lines up. Without a DMARC policy of quarantine or reject, receivers decide on their own.

From memory: say what each of SPF, DKIM and DMARC checks, and the DNS name each one lives at. (then click to check)

SPF: is the sending server's IP allowed? TXT at the domain itself. DKIM: is the signature valid and the message unchanged? Public key at selector._domainkey.domain. DMARC: what to do when they fail, and where to send reports. TXT at _dmarc.domain.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

Why can't the bare domain (example.com) usually be a CNAME, and which record does Outlook use to find its settings? (lesson 0012, 1 day ago)

A name with a CNAME can't hold any other records, and the bare domain needs MX and TXT. Outlook uses the autodiscover CNAME.

A syslog message arrives marked severity 0, another marked severity 6. Which is more urgent, and which port and transport does classic syslog use? (lesson 0010, 3 days ago)

Severity 0 (emergency): lower numbers are more severe; 6 is informational. Classic syslog uses UDP 514.

Which three 2.4 GHz channels don't overlap in the US, and why does 5 GHz usually lose more signal through walls? (lesson 0006, 7 days ago)

Channels 1, 6 and 11. Higher frequencies are absorbed more by walls and travel less far, so 5 GHz (and 6 GHz) trade range for speed.
