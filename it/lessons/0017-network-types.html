Network types: from your desk to the other side of the world

About 10 minutes. The exam sorts networks mostly by how far they reach: a few meters (PAN), a building (LAN), a city (MAN), the world (WAN). Two more are defined by what they're for: wireless access (WLAN) and storage (SAN). This is a vocabulary lesson, so the win is quick: name the type from a one-line description.[1]

Why this matters at work

"It's fast in the office and slow from home" makes more sense once you see the user went from a LAN to a WAN. Local links are usually the fastest; every step farther out usually means less throughput and more delay.[1] That's a network fact, not a broken account.

By reach: smallest to largest

TypeReachLooks like
PAN (personal area network)Around one person, a few metersBluetooth headset, smartwatch, phone tapping an NFC reader. Bluetooth, infrared and NFC are PAN technologies (lesson 0009).[1]
LAN (local area network)A floor, a building, a group of nearby buildingsThe office switches and the PCs on them. Usually the fastest links you'll have.[1]
MAN (metropolitan area network)A cityBetween a LAN and a WAN in size: for example, a city government linking its buildings across town, or a provider's fiber ring around a metro area.[1]
WAN (wide area network)Other cities, countries, the worldLinks between offices, and the internet itself. Can run over land-based links (fiber, copper) or satellites.[1] The site-to-site VPN from lesson 0016 runs over a WAN.

By purpose: WLAN and SAN

  • WLAN (wireless LAN): a LAN built on 802.11 Wi-Fi (lesson 0007). Users move around and keep their connection; more access points stretch it across a whole campus.[1] It's still a LAN in reach. "W" here means wireless, not wide.
  • SAN (storage area network): a dedicated network of storage that servers treat like a local disk. It gives block-level access: the server sees raw blocks and formats the volume itself.[1][2] SANs are usually kept on their own isolated network with very fast links, running Fibre Channel or iSCSI (SCSI disk commands over IP).[2]

Don't mix up SAN and NAS. A NAS (network attached storage, a device) shares files by name over SMB or NFS, like the file share from lesson 0005. A SAN (a network) hands a server blocks, as if it were a disk inside it.[2]

Memory hooks

P-L-M-W grows outward: Person, Local building, Metro city, World. WLAN = LAN without cables. SAN = disk (blocks), NAS = share (files).

Try it (2 minutes)

Your own PC is often on three network types at once. In PowerShell, list its adapters:

Get-NetAdapter | Format-Table Name, InterfaceDescription, Status, LinkSpeed

Example output (yours will differ):

Name                         InterfaceDescription                     Status       LinkSpeed
----                         --------------------                     ------       ---------
Ethernet                     Intel(R) Ethernet Connection             Up           1 Gbps
Wi-Fi                        Intel(R) Wi-Fi 6E AX211 160MHz           Disconnected 0 bps
Bluetooth Network Connection Bluetooth Device (Personal Area Network) Disconnected 3 Mbps

Ethernet is your LAN, Wi-Fi your WLAN, and Windows literally names the Bluetooth adapter Personal Area Network. Compare the LAN link speed with what a speed test to the internet (the WAN) gives you.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user's wireless earbuds keep dropping audio during calls whenever they walk across the room away from the laptop. Which network type is involved?

Bluetooth earbuds and a laptop form a personal area network: a few meters around one person. Walking away stretches the short Bluetooth range. A WLAN is Wi-Fi (802.11), which covers a whole floor or campus.

A city connects its library, police station and town hall, all a few kilometers apart, with its own high-speed fiber. Which term fits best?

A metropolitan area network spans a city: bigger than one building or campus (LAN), smaller than links between cities and countries (WAN).

A database server needs storage that it can format with its own file system and that behaves exactly like a local disk, but lives on a separate, fast network. What is being described?

A SAN presents storage as raw blocks, so the server formats and uses it like an internal disk. A NAS instead shares files by name; the NAS owns the file system. That block-level access is why databases and virtualization like SANs.

A remote worker says opening big files from the office file server is quick at their desk but slow from home, and their account and permissions are fine. What's the most likely reason?

At the desk the file travels over the LAN, usually the fastest link. From home it crosses the internet (a WAN) and often a VPN, so throughput drops and delay rises. Nothing is wrong with the account; the path is just longer and narrower.

From memory: put PAN, WAN, LAN and MAN in order from smallest to largest, with one example each. Then say what makes a SAN different from a NAS. (then click to check)

PAN (Bluetooth headset) → LAN (office building) → MAN (city's buildings linked across town) → WAN (offices in different cities; the internet). A SAN gives servers block-level storage that acts like a local disk, on its own fast network; a NAS shares files over SMB or NFS.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

Staff PCs can't print to a printer that was moved into its own VLAN. Why, and what has to be added? (lesson 0016, yesterday)

Each VLAN is a separate broadcast domain (and subnet), so traffic between VLANs must go through a router or layer 3 switch, with rules that allow staff → printer traffic.

Many devices send their log messages to one central server. What's that server role called, and which port and transport does it traditionally use? (lesson 0010, 7 days ago)

A syslog server, traditionally on UDP 514. Each message carries a severity, from 0 (emergency) to 7 (debug).

Telnet and SSH both give you a remote command line. Which ports do they use, and why should you pick SSH? (lesson 0003, 14 days ago)

Telnet: TCP 23, everything (including the password) in clear text. SSH: TCP 22, encrypted. Use SSH.
