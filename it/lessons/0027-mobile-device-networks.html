Cells, SIMs and satellites: the networks a phone lives on

About 10 minutes. A phone talks to several networks at once: cellular for voice and data, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, and it listens to GPS satellites.[1] You already know Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and hotspots (lessons 0007, 0009, 0025). The win today: the cellular generations, what a SIM really identifies, and why "where the phone is" and "where the sign-in came from" are two different answers.

Why this matters at work

A sign-in log shows a location worked out from the IP address, not from the phone's GPS. On cellular data, that IP often belongs to the carrier's gateway, which can be a city or two away (lesson 0018). So "sign-in from a strange city" on a phone is often just the carrier. Where you do need the device's real position (a lost company phone), MDM tools such as Intune's Locate device use the device's own location services, and only on supported, corporate-managed devices with location turned on.[4]

Cellular: cells and generations

Carriers split land into cells, each served by an antenna; your phone hands off from cell to cell as you move. The cellular network carries both voice and data. Airplane mode turns off all the radios (you can usually switch Wi-Fi and Bluetooth back on by hand).[1]

GenerationWhat to know
3GAround 1998 onward. Enough data throughput for real mobile data, which made phone GPS and maps practical.[1]
4G / LTE"Long Term Evolution". One converged standard replacing the old split between the two 3G families (GSM and CDMA). Messer cites up to about 150 Mbps.[1]
5GArrived around 2020; much faster (peaks in the gigabits) with lower latency, and built for many devices at once, including IoT.[1] Two flavors:
Sub-6 GHz: wide coverage, typically hundreds of Mbps.
mmWave: very high frequency, multi-gigabit peaks, but short range and blocked by walls and trees.[2]

Same physics as Wi-Fi bands (lesson 0006): higher frequency means more speed and less reach.

The SIM: who you are to the carrier

The SIM (Subscriber Identity Module) holds the subscription that lets a phone join the carrier's network. It can be a physical card or an eSIM: a chip soldered into the phone that downloads one or more carrier profiles, so switching carriers needs no card swap.[3] Four numbers get confused on tickets:

NumberIdentifies
IMEIThe phone itself (the hardware). Stays the same when you change SIMs.
ICCIDThe SIM (card or eSIM profile). Printed on a physical SIM.
IMSIThe subscriber: the account or line on the carrier's side.
EIDThe eSIM chip; carriers ask for it to send an eSIM profile.

Source for the table: [3]. The phone number itself is a separate thing again, assigned to the line.

GPS: the phone only listens

GPS is a constellation of at least 24 satellites. A receiver measures how long each satellite's signal took to arrive, turns that into a distance, and combines distances: at least four satellites give latitude, longitude and altitude, the fourth correcting the receiver's own clock error.[5] The phone never transmits to the satellites. Indoors, where satellites are hard to hear, phones fill in with nearby Wi-Fi networks and cell towers, which is why location gets less precise inside buildings.

Memory hook

4G = LTE; 5G = sub-6 (far) or mmWave (fast, near). IMEI = the phone, ICCID = the SIM, IMSI = the subscriber, EID = the eSIM chip. GPS = listen to four satellites. Sign-in location = the IP, not GPS.

Try it (2 minutes)

On your own phone, find its identifiers (don't share them anywhere):

  • iPhone: Settings → General → About. Scroll down: IMEI, ICCID and, if it supports eSIM, EID.
  • Android: Settings → About phone (sometimes under Status or SIM status). Dialing *#06# shows the IMEI on most phones.

Notice which numbers would change if you moved to another carrier (ICCID, IMSI) and which wouldn't (IMEI, EID).

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user is moving their company line to a new phone using eSIM. The carrier's support asks for a number that identifies the new phone's eSIM chip so they can send the profile. Which number?

The EID identifies the eSIM chip itself, so the carrier uses it to deliver a profile to that exact phone. The ICCID names a SIM or profile, the IMSI names the subscriber, and the IMEI names a phone's hardware (and the old phone isn't the target anyway).

A phone shows "5G" and runs a speed test at over 1 Gbps on a street corner, then drops to a few hundred Mbps after the user walks into an office building. What's the likely reason?

Multi-gigabit 5G comes from mmWave, which has short range and is easily blocked by walls and even trees. Indoors the phone falls back to sub-6 GHz 5G (or LTE): wider coverage, lower speed. A seated SIM or airplane mode would cut service, not just slow it.

A sign-in log shows a user's phone signing in from a city 300 km away, but the user is at their desk and Wi-Fi is off on the phone. What's the most likely explanation?

Sign-in locations come from the public IP address, not GPS. On cellular data, the IP belongs to the carrier's gateway, which often geolocates to another city. Check the device and the user's other sign-ins before assuming compromise; GPS and location services play no part in that log entry.

Why does a phone's GPS need signals from at least four satellites for a reliable 3D position?

Distance comes from signal travel time, so the receiver's imprecise clock is a fourth unknown alongside latitude, longitude and altitude. Four measurements solve all four. GPS receivers only listen; they never transmit to satellites.

From memory: what each of IMEI, ICCID, IMSI and EID identifies, and the trade-off between 5G sub-6 and mmWave. (then click to check)

IMEI: the phone. ICCID: the SIM (or eSIM profile). IMSI: the subscriber/line. EID: the eSIM chip. Sub-6: wide coverage, hundreds of Mbps. mmWave: gigabit speeds, short range, blocked by walls.

A user's webcam shows only a black image, but Windows lists the camera as working and the meeting app has permission. What do you check first, physically? (lesson 0024, 3 days ago)

A privacy shutter (or a camera kill key/switch). A closed shutter gives a black image while the device itself reports OK.

A switch and a router both forward traffic. What does each one look at to decide where a frame or packet goes? (lesson 0020, 7 days ago)

A switch forwards frames by destination MAC address inside one network. A router forwards packets by destination IP address between networks (subnets).

A domain's DMARC record says p=none. What happens to a spoofed message that fails SPF and DKIM, and what's that setting good for? (lesson 0013, 14 days ago)

It's delivered anyway; p=none only monitors. It's the safe starting point: collect the aggregate (rua) reports, fix legitimate senders, then move to quarantine and reject.
