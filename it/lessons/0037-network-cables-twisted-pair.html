Twisted, shielded, buried or in the ceiling: network cables

About 10 minutes. The Hardware domain moves from screens to cables. Today is the copper Ethernet cable: why its wires are twisted, when it needs a shield, which category carries which speed, and the two special jackets, direct burial and plenum.[1] The win: read the text printed on a cable's jacket and know where that cable is allowed to go.

Why this matters at work

"My wired connection is slow" sometimes has nothing to do with the network or the account: the PC linked at 100 Mbps instead of 1 Gbps because of an old or damaged patch cable. And when an office gets new cabling, someone has to know that the cable above the ceiling tiles isn't the same as the cheap one in the box under the desk.

1. Twisted pair: why the twists

An Ethernet cable holds four pairs of copper wires, eight wires in all, and each pair is twisted around itself. The two wires in a pair carry the same signal in opposite polarity. Any electrical noise that hits the cable hits both wires about equally, and because the receiver looks at the difference between them, the noise cancels out. Each pair has a slightly different twist rate, which keeps the pairs from picking up each other's signal (crosstalk).[1][2]

2. Unshielded vs. shielded

TypeWhat it hasUse it
UTP (unshielded twisted pair)Only the twists; no shielding around the pairs or wiresAlmost everywhere: offices, homes, patch cables. Cheaper, thinner, easier to bend
STP (shielded twisted pair)A foil or braided shield inside the jacket, around the pairs and/or the whole bundleElectrically noisy places: near motors, factory floors, heavy power lines. The shield must be grounded to work

3. Categories: speed and distance

The category (Cat) is printed on the jacket. Higher category means the cable is built to carry higher frequencies, so it supports faster Ethernet. Copper Ethernet's standard maximum run is 100 meters (about 328 feet).[3]

CategoryEthernetMax distance
Cat 5100 Mbps (legacy)100 m
Cat 5e1 Gbps (1000BASE-T)100 m
Cat 61 Gbps at 100 m; 10 Gbps only up to 55 m100 m / 55 m
Cat 6A10 Gbps (10GBASE-T)100 m

The exam's favorite contrast: Cat 6 does 10 Gbps, but only to 55 m; Cat 6A ("augmented") does 10 Gbps the full 100 m.[3]

4. Special jackets: buried and in the ceiling

Direct burial cable goes straight into the ground with no conduit, for example between two buildings. It has a tough, waterproof jacket (often gel-filled to keep water out) and is usually shielded; the shield helps against interference and can be used to ground the cable.[1]

A plenum is a space that moves air for heating and cooling, most often the gap above a drop ceiling or under a raised floor. In a fire, air in the plenum spreads smoke through the whole building. Ordinary cable has a PVC jacket, which gives off toxic smoke when it burns. Plenum-rated cable uses a fire-resistant, low-smoke jacket (such as FEP or low-smoke PVC). It's safer, but stiffer and costlier.[1][4]

On the jacket, look for CMP (communications plenum: required in air-handling spaces) or CMR (riser: vertical runs between floors). CMP can replace CMR, never the other way around. Local fire code decides the details.[4]

Memory hook

Twists cancel noise; shields block more of it (if grounded). 5e = 1 gig. 6 = 10 gig to 55. 6A = 10 gig to 100. Ground = direct burial. Ceiling air = plenum (CMP).

Try it (3 minutes)

  1. Find any Ethernet cable and read the text printed along its jacket. Look for the category (CAT5E, CAT6), UTP or STP/FTP, and a fire rating such as CM, CMR or CMP.
  2. On a wired Windows PC, check what speed the link actually negotiated: 
    Get-NetAdapter | Where-Object Status -eq Up |
  Format-Table Name, InterfaceDescription, LinkSpeed
    A gigabit network card on a gigabit switch showing 100 Mbps points at the cable (a damaged pair or an old Cat 5 patch cable) or the port.
A new hire's desktop shows LinkSpeed 100 Mbps while every neighbor shows 1 Gbps on the same switch. Cheapest first step? (then click to check)

Swap the patch cable for a known-good Cat 5e or Cat 6 one. Gigabit Ethernet needs all four pairs; one broken pair, or a cable that isn't rated for it, makes the link fall back to 100 Mbps, which needs only two pairs. If the speed stays at 100, try another wall or switch port.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A company wants 10 Gbps copper Ethernet to a wiring closet 90 meters away. Which cable category is the minimum that meets this?

Cat 6A carries 10GBASE-T the full 100 m. Cat 6 manages 10 Gbps only up to about 55 m, and Cat 5e tops out at 1 Gbps.

Network cable must run through the space above a drop ceiling that the building uses for return air. Which cable should you install?

An air-handling space is a plenum, so the cable needs a plenum (CMP) rating: its jacket resists fire and gives off little toxic smoke. PVC and riser-rated cable aren't allowed there.

Workstations on a factory floor next to large electric motors keep dropping their wired connections. The cables are new Cat 6 UTP. What's the best change?

Motors create electromagnetic interference. Shielded twisted pair, with the shield properly grounded, blocks far more of it than the twists alone. A plenum jacket is about fire safety, not noise.

Why are the two wires in each pair of an Ethernet cable twisted together?

Noise hits both wires of a twisted pair about equally; the receiver reads the difference between them, so the noise cancels. Different twist rates per pair also cut crosstalk between pairs.

From memory: UTP vs. STP in one line each, the speed and distance for Cat 5e, Cat 6 and Cat 6A, and what direct burial and plenum cable are for. (then click to check)

UTP: twists only, used almost everywhere. STP: adds a grounded shield for noisy places. Cat 5e 1 Gbps / 100 m; Cat 6 1 Gbps / 100 m or 10 Gbps / 55 m; Cat 6A 10 Gbps / 100 m. Direct burial: waterproof, usually shielded, straight into the ground. Plenum (CMP): fire-resistant, low-smoke jacket for air-handling spaces.

Before testing a risky update in a VM, what do you take so you can roll back in seconds? And in VM terms, what are the host and the guest? (lesson 0030, 7 days ago)

Take a snapshot: it saves the VM's state so you can revert if the update breaks something. The host is the real machine; the guest is the operating system running inside the VM; the hypervisor sits between them.

A battery report shows design capacity 50,000 mWh and full charge capacity 30,000 mWh. What's the battery's health, and what two drive form factors could replace an old laptop hard drive? (lesson 0023, 14 days ago)

Health = full charge ÷ design = 60%; worn enough to plan a replacement. An SSD in the 2.5-inch form factor (drop-in for the old drive) or an M.2 card, if the laptop has an M.2 slot.

Which 802.11 standard works only on 5 GHz, and what's its Wi-Fi generation name? (lesson 0007, 30 days ago)

802.11ac, called Wi-Fi 5: it's the 5 GHz-only one. 802.11n (Wi-Fi 4) and 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6) use both 2.4 and 5 GHz.
