How to take it

No scrolling back to the lessons until you've answered. A wrong answer followed by the explanation sticks better than a right answer you looked up. Each explanation links to the lesson that taught it, so you know exactly what to reread.

The network team creates a new VLAN, on its own subnet, for the security cameras. Every camera on those ports now shows a 169.254.x.x address; devices on the old VLAN are fine. What's the most likely cause?

Each VLAN is its own subnet and broadcast domain, and DHCP needs one scope per subnet (plus a relay on the router if the server sits elsewhere). No answer to the Discover means APIPA. The cameras clearly have power, since they're asking for an address, and 802.1Q tags are how a trunk is supposed to work. See lesson 0016 and lesson 0014.

A user reads email in a desktop app on their laptop and on their phone. Messages opened on the laptop disappear from the phone and from webmail. The laptop app is set to fetch mail on port 110. What's going on?

Port 110 is POP3: "post office", collect the mail and take it home, usually removing it from the server. The fix is to set the laptop app to IMAP (143, or 993 with TLS), which keeps mail on the server and syncs every device. SMTP only sends. See lesson 0004.

Security wants every website a company laptop visits to pass through the office's web filtering, even when the user works from home on the VPN. Which VPN setting delivers that?

Full tunnel sends all traffic through the VPN, web browsing included, so the office's filtering sees it. Split tunnel sends only company traffic through and lets the rest go straight to the internet (SplitTunneling : True in Get-VpnConnection). Site-to-site joins whole offices, not one laptop. See lesson 0016.

A Windows server answers ping, but Remote Desktop to it times out. You suspect a firewall. Which test checks exactly the thing that's failing?

Ping proves the machine is reachable; the next question is the port. RDP listens on TCP 3389, and Test-NetConnection server -Port 3389 shows TcpTestSucceeded True or False. 443 is HTTPS, 22 is SSH, and 53 is DNS (mostly UDP, which this test can't check anyway). See lesson 0002 and lesson 0003.

With a toner probe you found which closet cable runs to desk 14. The PC at desk 14 still shows no link light, even with a known-good patch cable. Which tool do you reach for next?

The toner finds a cable; it can beep happily on one with a broken pair. Now you know which cable it is, so test it: a cable tester checks every pin end to end. If a pair is open at the jack or panel, re-terminate it with a punchdown tool (not a crimper: that's for plugs on loose ends). See lesson 0019.

A database server needs more storage. It must see the storage as a raw disk it formats itself, over a fast network kept apart from user traffic. What do you set up?

A SAN hands a server raw blocks, as if it were a disk inside it, usually on its own isolated high-speed network (Fibre Channel or iSCSI). A NAS shares files by name over SMB or NFS, so the server can't format it. SAN = disk, NAS = share. See lesson 0017.

A laptop on 5 GHz shows one bar in a far meeting room, two walls from the access point. The same network name is also offered on 2.4 GHz. Why might 2.4 GHz work better in that room?

Low frequency = long range: 2.4 GHz goes farther and through walls better, at the cost of speed. It's the opposite of the other three: 2.4 GHz has only three clean channels (1, 6, 11), is the slower band, and shares the air with Bluetooth. See lesson 0006.

A 20-person branch office needs constant access to file and app servers at headquarters. Nobody at the branch should have to start any VPN software. What fits best?

Site-to-site connects the two offices' firewalls (or concentrators) and stays up all the time; users run nothing. Client-to-site, even set to Always On, needs a VPN client on each device. VLANs divide a local network; they don't join distant ones. See lesson 0016.

A new access point needs about 25 W at the device. The closet switch only supports 802.3af PoE. What happens, and what's the fix?

802.3af guarantees only about 13 W at the device. 802.3at (PoE+) guarantees about 25.5 W, and 802.3bt (PoE++) more. Use a PoE+ switch, or a PoE+ injector for this one AP. Patch panels are passive and never supply power. See lesson 0021.

An internal line-of-business app is switched from LDAP to LDAPS so its directory lookups are encrypted. Now it can't reach the directory server at all. The firewall rules weren't touched. Which port is most likely blocked?

LDAPS uses its own port, TCP 636 (encrypted from the first byte), just as HTTPS moved from 80 to 443. A firewall that only ever allowed 389 lets plain LDAP through and silently drops 636. See lesson 0005.

A user gets an "unusual sign-in location" alert placing them in a city 300 miles away. They were working from a café over their phone's hotspot and approved the MFA prompt themselves. What's the most likely explanation?

Mobile providers hand out IP addresses from central pools, so the location shown for a cellular IP is only a guess and is often far from the device. Check the sign-in log's details and confirm with the user, but a hotspot plus their own MFA approval fits. A MAC address never leaves the local network, so it can't move a sign-in location. See lesson 0018.

The office wants its VoIP phones on their own VLAN. The current switch is plug and play: no settings page, nothing to log in to. What's needed?

An unmanaged switch can't be configured, so it can't assign ports to VLANs. A managed switch adds VLANs, traffic prioritization (handy for voice), redundancy and port mirroring. Rule of thumb: anyone needs VLANs, the switch must be managed. A hub is obsolete and makes no decisions at all. See lesson 0020.

An audit finds the team still manages some older switches over Telnet. What should replace it, and why?

Telnet (23) and SSH (22) do the same job, a text console on a remote device, but Telnet sends everything, passwords included, in the clear. SSH encrypts the whole session. HTTP and FTP are unencrypted too, and RDP is for Windows desktops, not switch consoles. See lesson 0003.

Two coworkers in the same rural town have the same DSL plan. One, close to the provider's central office, gets full speed; the other, several miles out, gets a fraction of it. What's the most likely cause?

DSL = distance: it runs over phone lines and slows the farther you are from the central office, with a limit around 10,000 feet. Rain fade is satellite, a shared node is cable, and data caps are cellular. See lesson 0018.

example.com has two MX records: 10 mail1.example.com and 20 mail2.example.com. mail1 is down for maintenance. What happens to incoming mail?

MX priority: lower wins. Senders try 10 (mail1) first and, when it doesn't answer, move on to 20 (mail2). That's exactly why domains publish a second MX. See lesson 0012.

Explain it in your own words

Say or write the answer before you open each one. If you can explain it to a coworker, you know it.

A coworker says "VLAN and VPN are basically the same thing." Explain in two sentences why they do opposite jobs. (lesson 0016)

A VLAN divides: it splits one physical switch into several separate networks (broadcast domains), and traffic between them must go through a router. A VPN connects: it joins devices or offices that are far apart through an encrypted tunnel over the internet, so they act as if they were on the same network.

Switch, router, access point, firewall: what does each one look at to decide where traffic goes? And why does a home "wireless router" count as several of them? (lesson 0020)

Switch and access point: the destination MAC address (same network). Router: the IP address (between networks; your default gateway). Firewall: its rules, such as ports or applications. A home "wireless router" is a router, a small switch, an access point and a firewall in one box.

Why does DHCP have to use UDP rather than TCP, and what are ports 67 and 68 for? (lesson 0004, lesson 0002)

A device asking for an address doesn't have one yet, so it can't set up a TCP conversation; it just broadcasts a UDP "is there a DHCP server out there?" The server listens on 67, the client on 68.

Name the box that terminates coax, a phone line and fiber. Then explain what "demarcation point" means for who you call when the internet is down. (lesson 0021, lesson 0018)

Coax: cable modem (DOCSIS). Phone line: DSL modem. Fiber: ONT. The ONT is the demarc: the provider owns everything up to and including it, you own what plugs into it. A fault on their side means you call the provider; on your side, it's yours to fix.
