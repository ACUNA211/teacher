Eight wires, two orders: T568A and T568B

About 10 minutes. Yesterday's cable had four twisted pairs. Today: which color goes on which pin of the RJ45 plug. There are two standard orders, T568A and T568B, and they differ in just two pairs.[1] The win: look through the clear plastic of a plug and tell which standard it uses, and whether the cable is straight-through or crossover.

Why this matters at work

Most cables come pre-made, but wall jacks and patch panels are wired by hand, and so is the odd cable someone crimped themselves. A jack wired with the pairs in the wrong order can still show a link light yet run slowly, drop out, or not connect at all. Knowing the color order lets you spot that in seconds instead of blaming the PC or the user's account.

1. Why there's a standard at all

An Ethernet cable has eight wires and an RJ45 plug has eight pins. The colors are standardized so that every technician, in every building, puts the same wire on the same pin, and a cable or jack wired by one person works with one wired by someone else.[1] Each pair has a solid color and a white-striped partner: orange, green, blue, brown.

2. The two orders

Pin 1 is on the left when you hold the plug with the gold contacts facing you, the clip on the back, and the cable hanging down.[2]

PinT568AT568B
1White-greenWhite-orange
2GreenOrange
3White-orangeWhite-green
4BlueBlue
5White-blueWhite-blue
6OrangeGreen
7White-brownWhite-brown
8BrownBrown

The only difference: the green and orange pairs trade places (pins 1, 2, 3 and 6). Blue (4, 5) and brown (7, 8) never move.[2][3] Notice the green or orange pair is split: it sits on pins 3 and 6, wrapped around the blue pair.

Which one to use? Electrically they work the same. Many organizations use 568B, others use 568A;[1] what matters is that a site picks one and uses it on every jack, panel and cable.

3. Straight-through vs. crossover

CableEndsUsed for
Straight-through (patch cable)Same standard on both ends: B–B (or A–A)Almost everything: PC to wall jack, switch to router, patch panel to switch
CrossoverOne end A, the other end BHistorically, linking two devices of the same kind directly: PC to PC, switch to switch

Why crossover worked: in 10 and 100 Mbps Ethernet, one device sends on pins 1–2 and receives on 3–6. Two PCs joined straight-through would both talk on the same pins. Swapping orange and green at one end (A on one side, B on the other) moves pins 1↔3 and 2↔6, so one side's transmit lands on the other's receive.[4]

Today this rarely matters. Modern network ports support Auto-MDIX: the port detects how the other end is wired and swaps its own transmit and receive pairs if needed, so a straight-through cable works between any two devices.[4][5] Gigabit Ethernet uses all four pairs in both directions, which is another reason a damaged pair hurts it.[5] Some older or industrial gear lacks Auto-MDIX, which is where a crossover can still show up.[4]

Memory hook

B starts with orange; A starts with green. The pattern is the same: white-X, X, white-Y, blue, white-blue, Y, white-brown, brown. Blue and brown never move. Same on both ends = straight-through. A on one, B on the other = crossover. Auto-MDIX makes crossovers mostly unnecessary.

Try it (3 minutes)

  1. Pick up any patch cable. Hold one plug with the gold contacts facing you and the clip at the back. Read the colors left to right through the clear plastic. White-orange first? That's T568B. White-green first? T568A.
  2. Check the other end the same way. Same order on both ends means a straight-through cable.
A hand-wired wall jack is 568B and the patch cable is 568B, but the keystone on the other side of the wall was punched down 568A. What do you have, and does it matter? (then click to check)

End to end, that link is effectively a crossover (B on one end, A on the other). With Auto-MDIX on both devices it will probably still connect, but it breaks the site's standard and will confuse the next person who troubleshoots it. Re-terminate the odd jack so the whole run uses one standard.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

Looking through a plug with the contacts facing you, the wires read white-orange, orange, white-green, blue, white-blue, green, white-brown, brown. Which standard is this?

White-orange on pin 1 and orange on pin 2 is T568B. T568A starts with white-green, green. You can't tell straight-through or crossover from one end alone.

A technician is converting a T568A termination to T568B. Which wires need to move?

The two standards differ only in the green and orange pairs, on pins 1, 2, 3 and 6. Blue (pins 4–5) and brown (pins 7–8) stay put.

A cable has T568A on one end and T568B on the other. What kind of cable is it?

A on one end and B on the other swaps pins 1↔3 and 2↔6: that's a crossover. It was used to link two devices of the same kind directly. Straight-through has the same standard on both ends.

A technician links two modern switches directly with an ordinary straight-through patch cable, and the link comes up fine. Why?

Auto-MDIX detects how the other end is wired and swaps transmit and receive internally, so a straight-through cable works switch to switch. Without it, you'd need a crossover.

From memory: write the T568B order for pins 1 to 8, say what changes for T568A, and define straight-through, crossover and Auto-MDIX in one line each. (then click to check)

T568B: white-orange, orange, white-green, blue, white-blue, green, white-brown, brown. T568A: swap the orange and green pairs (white-green, green, white-orange, blue, white-blue, orange, white-brown, brown). Straight-through: same standard on both ends. Crossover: A on one end, B on the other. Auto-MDIX: the port detects the wiring and swaps pairs itself, so straight-through works everywhere.
