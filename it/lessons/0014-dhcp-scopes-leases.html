DHCP scopes and leases: who gets which address, and for how long

About 10 minutes. You already know what DHCP does (hands out the four IP settings, lesson 0001) and which ports it uses (UDP 67/68, lesson 0004). Today: how the server is set up. Five words cover it: scope, pool, exclusion, reservation, lease.[1]

Why this matters at work

"The printer stopped working after the weekend" (its address changed) and "new laptops get a 169.254 address but older ones are fine" (the server ran out of addresses) are both DHCP configuration problems, not broken hardware. Knowing the five words tells you which setting to ask the network team about.

The five words

Example network: 192.168.10.0/24, a private range like the ones from lesson 0001.

TermWhat it isExample
ScopeThe whole DHCP setup for one subnet: its address range plus the settings handed out with it. One scope per subnet.[2]"Office LAN" scope for 192.168.10.0/24
PoolThe addresses the server may actually hand out..1–.254, minus exclusions
ExclusionAddresses carved out of the range so DHCP never offers them. Used for devices you set by hand (router, servers).[2].1–.20 excluded
ReservationA fixed pairing of one MAC address to one IP. The device still uses DHCP, but always gets the same address.[3]Printer AA-BB-… → .50
LeaseHow long a device may keep its address before it must renew. Unrenewed addresses go back into the pool.[3]8 days

The scope also carries scope options: the other settings that ride along with the address. The important ones are the three you met in lesson 0001: default gateway (the "router" option), DNS servers, and the subnet mask, plus a DNS domain name.[3] A wrong DNS option in the scope means every device on that subnet gets the wrong DNS server at once.

One server can run scopes for many subnets, even though the Discover broadcast stops at the router. The router forwards it as a DHCP relay (Cisco calls this an "IP helper"), so the server knows which subnet's scope to answer from.[3]

How a lease renews

  • Getting one: four messages, remembered as DORA: Discover (client broadcasts), Offer (server proposes an address), Request (client asks for it), Acknowledge (server confirms).[3]
  • At 50% of the lease (called T1), the client asks the same server to extend it. Usually it says yes and the clock restarts.[4]
  • At 87.5% (T2, seven-eighths), if the first server never answered, the client broadcasts to any DHCP server.[4]
  • At 100%, the address is gone. The client starts DORA again, and if nobody answers, Windows falls back to APIPA (169.254.x.x).

Lease length is a trade-off. Long leases (days) mean less traffic and stable addresses. Short leases (an hour or two) recycle addresses quickly, which suits busy guest Wi-Fi where phones come and go.

Memory hooks

Exclusion = "never give out" (for things set by hand). Reservation = "always give this one to that MAC" (for things that should stay put but still use DHCP). Renew at half, beg anyone at seven-eighths.

Try it (2 minutes)

On a Windows PC, run:[5]

ipconfig /all

Find your active adapter and read these lines:

   DHCP Enabled. . . . . . . . . . . : Yes
   Lease Obtained. . . . . . . . . . : Friday, October 9, 2026 8:02:11 AM
   Lease Expires . . . . . . . . . . : Saturday, October 10, 2026 8:02:11 AM
   DHCP Server . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.10.1

Subtract the two dates: that's the lease length (one day in this example, so the PC will try to renew around 8 PM tonight). Run ipconfig /renew and look again: Lease Obtained jumps to now. Your values will differ; a home router often uses a lease of a day.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A network printer gets its address from DHCP. After a long power outage it comes back with a different IP, and everyone's print jobs fail. What's the best fix?

A reservation ties the printer's MAC address to one IP, so DHCP always hands it the same address. An exclusion does the opposite: DHCP would never give that address out, so the printer couldn't get it from DHCP at all.

A scope has about 100 addresses and an 8-day lease. During a busy visitor week, newly arrived laptops get 169.254.x.x while devices already on the network work fine. Most likely cause?

Visitors' phones and laptops hold their addresses for 8 days even after they leave, so the pool is used up. Devices already holding a lease keep working; new ones get no offer and fall back to APIPA. Fixes: a bigger scope or a shorter lease. A wrong DNS or gateway option wouldn't cause a 169.254 address.

The scope's range is .1–.254. The router at .1 and a file server at .10 have addresses typed in by hand. What should you add so DHCP never hands those addresses to another device?

Devices with static (hand-typed) addresses don't talk to DHCP, so the server doesn't know they exist. Exclude their addresses from the range, or DHCP may hand the same IP to a laptop and cause an address conflict. Only one scope is allowed per subnet.

A laptop received an 8-day lease on Monday morning. When does it first try to renew, and who does it ask?

Renewal starts at 50% of the lease (T1): day 4, sent to the server that gave the lease. Only if that fails does it broadcast to any server, at 87.5% (T2): day 7.

From memory: explain the difference between an exclusion and a reservation, and say when you'd use each. (then click to check)

Exclusion: addresses DHCP never offers. Use it for devices with hand-typed (static) addresses, like the router or a server. Reservation: one IP always given to one MAC address. Use it for devices that should keep the same address but still get their settings from DHCP, like printers.

Which of SPF, DKIM and DMARC uses a private/public key pair, and at which DNS name does the DMARC record live? (lesson 0013, 1 day ago)

DKIM: the sender signs with a private key; the public key sits at selector._domainkey.domain. DMARC is a TXT record at _dmarc.domain.

Name the four internet appliances and say what each one sits between. (lesson 0011, 3 days ago)

Spam gateway: internet ↔ mail server. UTM (all-in-one): internal network ↔ internet. Load balancer: users ↔ a group of servers. Proxy: users ↔ the internet, making requests for them.

A box says "Wi-Fi 6E". Which 802.11 letters is that, and what does the "E" add? (lesson 0007, 7 days ago)

802.11ax. Wi-Fi 6 uses 2.4 and 5 GHz; 6E adds the 6 GHz band.
