Tethers, hotspots and plugs: connecting a phone

About 10 minutes. The laptop is done; now the phone. A phone connects to other things in two ways: with a cable (USB or Lightning) or wirelessly (Bluetooth, NFC, Wi-Fi).[1] The win: knowing the difference between tethering and a hotspot, and what a laptop's network looks like when it borrows a phone's connection.

Why this matters at work

When a user's laptop rides on a phone's connection, its sign-ins come from the mobile carrier's public IP, not the office's (lesson 0018). A sign-in policy that trusts only office addresses will treat it as "outside", so expect an extra MFA prompt or a block. And on company-owned Android phones, Intune can switch sharing off entirely: its Tethering and access to hotspots setting set to Block stops both.[4]

Wired: three plugs to recognize

ConnectorWhere you'll see itReversible?
Micro-USB (and older mini-USB)Older Android phones, cheap accessoriesNo: one way up
LightningApple's own 8-pin plug: older iPhones and iPads[1]Yes
USB-CAlmost everything new: Android, iPhone 15 and later, laptops. 24 pins; carries data, power and video[1]Yes

Apple moved the iPhone from Lightning to USB-C in 2023 (iPhone 15), after the EU made USB-C the common charging port for phones.[5] So "my old cable doesn't fit my new phone" is usually Lightning vs. USB-C, not a broken port.

Wireless: Bluetooth and NFC

Bluetooth builds a PAN (personal area network, lesson 0017), about 10 m of range. Devices must pair once before they talk:[2]

  1. Turn Bluetooth on for both devices.
  2. Put the accessory into discoverable (pairing) mode, often by holding its power or Bluetooth button.
  3. Select it from the list on the phone or laptop.
  4. Confirm the matching code or PIN if asked, so you know it's the right device.
  5. Test it.

NFC (yesterday) is tap-to-connect: a few centimeters, used for payments, sharing, and starting a Bluetooth pairing without the steps above.[1]

Tethering vs. hotspot

Both share the phone's cellular internet. The difference is how many devices and over what link:[2]

TetheringHotspot
DevicesOne deviceSeveral devices
LinkUSB cable (or Bluetooth)The phone becomes a Wi-Fi access point
BonusOver USB, the phone charges tooNo cable; drains the phone's battery

Either can be limited or charged extra by the mobile carrier's plan.[2] Windows can also join a phone's hotspot over Bluetooth: Settings → Bluetooth & devices → the phone → Join Personal Area Network.[3]

Memory hook

Lightning = old Apple, 8 pins. USB-C = everything new, 24 pins, either way up. Pair: on, discoverable, select, confirm, test. Tether = one device by cable; hotspot = many devices by Wi-Fi.

Try it (3 minutes)

Next time you share your phone's connection with a laptop, run this in PowerShell:[6]

Get-NetAdapter | Where-Object Status -eq Up
ipconfig
  • USB tether: a new adapter appears. From an Android phone it's usually named Remote NDIS based Internet Sharing Device: the phone shows up as a wired network card.[7]
  • Hotspot: no new adapter; your normal Wi-Fi adapter joins the phone's network.

Either way, look at ipconfig's default gateway (lesson 0001): it's the phone. An iPhone hotspot, for example, typically hands out 172.20.10.x addresses with the phone at 172.20.10.1.[8] The phone is acting as a small router with its own DHCP server.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

Three coworkers at an offsite meeting need internet for their laptops. The venue has no Wi-Fi, but one of them has a phone with a good cellular signal. What's the best option?

Several devices sharing one phone's cellular connection is exactly what a hotspot does: the phone becomes a Wi-Fi access point. USB tethering serves only the one device plugged in. Headsets and NFC don't carry an internet connection.

A user upgrades from an iPhone 12 to an iPhone 16 and says the charging cable from their old phone "doesn't fit, so the new port must be broken". What's most likely?

Apple switched the iPhone from its 8-pin Lightning connector to USB-C starting with the iPhone 15 (2023). An iPhone 12 cable is Lightning, so it won't fit. Both connectors are reversible, so orientation isn't the issue, and MDM doesn't change a plug's shape.

A user's new Bluetooth headset never appears in the laptop's list of devices to add. Bluetooth is on and working on the laptop. What should they do next?

The laptop can only list devices that are discoverable. New headsets usually need a long press of the power or Bluetooth button to enter pairing (discoverable) mode. Wi-Fi drivers and hotspots have nothing to do with Bluetooth pairing.

On a company-owned, fully managed Android phone, the hotspot and USB tethering options are greyed out. The user's carrier plan includes tethering. What's the likely cause?

Greyed-out options on a managed phone point to device management. Intune's Android Enterprise restriction "Tethering and access to hotspots" set to Block turns off both. No signal wouldn't grey out the settings, and the cable and Bluetooth don't control the hotspot switch.

From memory: name the five Bluetooth pairing steps. Then: what does a USB-tethered Android phone look like to Windows? (then click to check)

Turn on both → make the accessory discoverable → select it → confirm the code/PIN → test. A USB-tethered Android phone appears as a new network adapter (Remote NDIS based Internet Sharing Device), and ipconfig shows the phone as the default gateway.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A company laptop's camera works in no app, Device Manager shows it OK, and the Windows camera switches are greyed out. What's the cause, and what's the first check if the picture were just black? (lesson 0024, 1 day ago)

Greyed out "managed by your organization" = a management policy (for example Intune's Allow Camera set to Not allowed). A black picture with the light on = the privacy shutter is closed.

Why can a sign-in from a laptop on a phone's connection appear to come from a city far away? (lesson 0018, 7 days ago)

On cellular, the public IP belongs to the mobile carrier, and carrier addresses often geolocate to a regional hub far from the device. The sign-in log records that IP, not the user's real location.

What's the difference between an embedded system and a SCADA system, and why do both usually sit on their own network segment? (lesson 0011, 14 days ago)

An embedded system is a purpose-built device whose software you can't easily change or patch. SCADA supervises and controls industrial equipment across a site. Both are hard to patch, so they're isolated on their own segment to limit what can reach them.
