Moving heat out: fans, heat sinks and liquid cooling

About 10 minutes. Every part you've met this week (CPU, graphics card, chipset, M.2 drive) turns power into heat. Messer's Cooling video names the tools that move it out: fans, passive cooling, heat sinks, thermal paste, thermal pads and liquid cooling.[1] The win: follow the heat from the chip to the room, and pick the right fix for a hot, loud or slow machine.

Why this matters at work

"My laptop gets slow in the afternoon" and "the fan is really loud" are often heat tickets, not software tickets. A CPU that runs too hot slows itself down (thermal throttling) to protect itself, so the user sees a slow PC, not an error. Before reimaging, ask where the laptop sits and look at the vents.

The idea: one path for the heat

Heat has to travel from the chip to the room. Each cooling part handles one step:

chip → thermal paste / pad → heat sink (fins) → air → fans → out of the case

PartWhat it doesRemember
Thermal pasteFills the microscopic gaps between the chip and the heat sink, so heat crosses instead of hitting trapped air[4]A thin layer, not a blob. Clean off the old paste and apply fresh when you remove a cooler
Thermal padA ready-cut pad that does the same job as pasteEasy and won't leak onto other parts; a bit less effective than paste; single use: replace it whenever the heat sink comes off[1]
Heat sinkMetal with many fins: a large surface that hands heat to the airOn its own it's passive cooling: no moving parts, silent; good for quiet devices like set-top boxes[1][2]
FansPush air through the heat sink and through the case (active cooling)Case fans come in standard sizes such as 80, 120 and 200 mm; graphics cards carry their own fans[1]
Liquid coolingA pump moves coolant from a block on the CPU (or GPU) to a radiator, where fans cool it, and back again[4]For when fans get too loud or can't keep up; usually a sealed closed loop ("all-in-one")[1]

Who controls the fans?

The motherboard. It has temperature sensors and a built-in fan controller, and you set its behavior in the BIOS/UEFI: maximum cooling, minimum noise, or full speed all the time.[3] That's why the fan headers you met in lesson 0054 come in a 4-pin (PWM) version: the fourth pin lets the board set the speed.

Case fans work as a team: the usual layout pulls cool air in at the front and pushes hot air out at the back (and top, since heat rises).[4]

When cooling fails

Messer's hardware troubleshooting video gives the checks: make sure fans are clean and not blocked, and that heat sinks have proper thermal paste. A rattle inside the case is often a loose heat sink.[5] Dust is the everyday villain: it clogs the fins, so the fans spin harder and louder while moving less heat.

Rule of thumb

Paste or pad fills the gap. Heat sink spreads it. Fans carry it away. Liquid when air isn't enough. Passive = no moving parts. A pad comes off, a new pad goes on.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. On a Windows PC with a dedicated graphics card, open Task Manager (Ctrl+Shift+Esc) → Performance → GPU. Look for GPU Temperature (in °C). It only appears for dedicated GPUs, and an old graphics driver can hide it.[6]
  2. Play a video or a demanding app for a minute and watch the number climb, then fall when you stop. That curve is the cooling system at work.
  3. On a desktop, hold a hand behind the case: warm air coming out the back means the exhaust fan is doing its job.
Before checking: a laptop with only integrated graphics shows no GPU Temperature line. Is its cooling broken? (then click to check)

No. Task Manager only shows that reading for a dedicated GPU.[6] Integrated graphics live inside the CPU (lesson 0062), so they share the CPU's heat sink and fan. A missing line is a reporting limit, not a fault.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A small PC will play videos in a quiet boardroom, and any fan noise is a problem. Which cooling fits best?

A heat sink on its own is passive cooling: no moving parts, so no noise. That's why quiet devices like set-top boxes use it.[1] Liquid cooling still has a pump and radiator fans, and thick paste insulates instead of helping.

A user's laptop gets slow after twenty minutes of video calls. The fan roars, the bottom is hot, and the laptop sits on a blanket. What's the likely cause?

A blanket blocks the intake vents. Heat builds, the fan speeds up, and the CPU slows itself down to stay safe: thermal throttling. Keep fans and vents clean and unobstructed.[5] A hard surface or a stand is the first fix.

A tech removes a CPU heat sink to reseat it. The heat sink used a thermal pad. What should they do before refitting it?

Thermal pads are single use: once the heat sink comes off, the pad must be replaced.[1] Bare metal on metal leaves microscopic air gaps, and air is a poor conductor of heat.

A video-editing workstation runs its air cooler's fans at full speed all day. It's loud and still runs hot under load. Which upgrade addresses both problems?

When fans get loud or can't keep up, liquid cooling is the next step: the coolant carries heat to a large radiator, so its fans can spin slower.[1] Pads perform a little worse than paste, and an open side panel breaks the planned front-to-back airflow.

From memory: trace the heat path from chip to room, naming the part at each step, and say which part is "passive". (then click to check)

Chip → thermal paste or pad (fills the gaps) → heat sink fins → air → fans push it out of the case. Or with liquid cooling: block → pump → radiator → fans. A heat sink with no fan is passive (no moving parts).

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A user installs a capture card and a new graphics card. Which one brings video in, and where does the monitor cable go? (lesson 0062, 1 day ago)

The capture card brings video in (camera, console). The monitor goes into the graphics card's ports, not the motherboard's, or the new card does nothing.

A company needs one secure place for a certificate authority's signing key, used by many servers. TPM or HSM, and why? (lesson 0060, 3 days ago)

HSM: a dedicated, hardened device that stores and uses keys centrally for many systems. A TPM protects keys for one device (BitLocker, Windows Hello).

A spare AMD AM4 processor is lying around. Can it go in a new AM5 board? And which socket style has the pins in the socket? (lesson 0056, 7 days ago)

No: each socket generation only takes its own CPUs, so AM4 won't fit AM5. LGA (land grid array) has the pins in the socket and flat pads on the CPU. (CPU coolers mount to the socket too, so check the cooler supports it.)
