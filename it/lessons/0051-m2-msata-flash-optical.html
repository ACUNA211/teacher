Small slots, pocket drives and discs: M.2, flash and optical

About 10 minutes. Lesson 0049 covered the drives (HDD vs. SSD) and the interfaces (SATA, SAS, NVMe). Today finishes the video: the small M.2 and mSATA slots, flash memory you carry around, and optical discs.[1] The win: look at an SSD listing and a motherboard manual and say whether the drive will work.

Why this matters at work

A USB flash drive is the easiest way for company data to walk out the door, or get lost in a parking lot. That's why managed Windows devices often get a policy that blocks removable storage or allows only reading from it (in Intune, a device restrictions or device control setting).[4] When a user says "my USB stick stopped working on my work laptop," the first question is whether it's broken or blocked on purpose.

1. M.2: one slot, two possible languages

M.2 is a small card-shaped drive that plugs straight into the motherboard: no cable. It's the usual SSD in laptops and modern desktops. The catch: an M.2 drive can speak SATA or NVMe, and they look almost the same.[1]

Read thisWhat it tells you
Key (notch in the edge connector)M key (one notch): up to four PCIe lanes, the usual NVMe drive. B+M key (two notches): usually an M.2 SATA drive.[2]
Size code, e.g. 2280Width then length in mm: 22 mm wide, 80 mm long. Others: 2230, 2242, 2260, 22110. It must fit the screw post.[3]
The motherboard manualWhich protocol the slot supports. Some M.2 slots are SATA only: an NVMe drive there won't be detected.[2]

mSATA is the older, smaller cousin: a little SATA-only card with a different connector. Messer calls it a stopgap between SATA and M.2; you'll find it in older laptops, and it doesn't fit an M.2 slot.[1][3]

2. Flash memory you carry

Flash is non-volatile: it keeps data with the power off (the same chips inside an SSD). The removable kinds: USB flash drives and memory cards like SD and microSD (cameras, phones, some laptops).[1]

Messer's warning: they're small, so they're easy to lose or damage, and not a good place for the only copy of anything (archival storage).[1]

3. Optical discs

A laser reads (and burns) data on a spinning disc. Rare on new PCs; when you need one, an external USB drive does the job.

DiscCapacity
CDAbout 700 MB
DVD4.7 GB single layer, 8.5 GB dual layer
Blu-ray25 GB single layer, 50 GB dual layer (BDXL goes to 100 GB and more)

Capacities from disc-format references.[5] Memory hook: each generation is roughly 5–7× the last (0.7 → 4.7 → 25).

Rule of thumb

M.2: check three things: the key, the length (2280), and whether the slot speaks SATA or NVMe. mSATA is older and won't fit M.2. Flash is handy but easy to lose. Optical: CD 700 MB, DVD 4.7/8.5 GB, Blu-ray 25/50 GB.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. Plug in a USB flash drive (or SD card) if you have one.
  2. In Windows PowerShell:
    Get-Volume | Format-Table DriveLetter, FileSystemLabel, DriveType, FileSystem, Size
  3. Read DriveType: your internal SSD shows Fixed, a flash drive or card shows Removable, an optical drive shows CD-ROM.
  4. Then Get-PhysicalDisk | Format-Table FriendlyName, BusType (from lesson 0049): the flash drive shows BusType USB, an M.2 NVMe SSD shows NVMe, an M.2 SATA SSD shows SATA.
A laptop's M.2 SSD shows BusType SATA. The user wants it faster. What do you check before buying an NVMe drive? (then click to check)

The laptop's manual: does the M.2 slot support NVMe (PCIe), or only SATA? Also the length it takes (often 2280). If the slot is SATA-only, an NVMe drive won't be detected at all.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A motherboard manual says its M.2 slot supports SATA only. A user installs an M-key NVMe SSD. What happens?

The drive only speaks NVMe and the slot only speaks SATA, so they can't talk: the drive isn't detected.[2] It doesn't fall back to SATA speed. Always check which protocol the slot supports.

A replacement SSD is listed as M.2 2280. What does 2280 tell you?

The M.2 size code is width then length in millimetres: 22 mm wide, 80 mm long.[3] It says nothing about capacity, speed or PCIe version; it tells you whether the drive reaches the screw post.

You need to hand a client a 20 GB video archive on a single disc. What's the smallest disc type that fits?

20 GB is more than any DVD holds (8.5 GB max), so you need Blu-ray: 25 GB on a single layer.[5]

A user keeps the only copy of last year's records on a USB flash drive in a desk drawer. What's the main problem?

Flash is non-volatile (it keeps data without power) and has no moving parts. The problem is that a small removable drive is easy to lose or damage, so it's a poor choice for archival storage.[1] Keep the records somewhere backed up, like company cloud storage.

From memory: the two M.2 keys and what each usually means, what "2280" means, why mSATA is different, and the capacities of CD, DVD and Blu-ray. (then click to check)

M key = usually NVMe (up to x4 PCIe); B+M key = usually SATA. 2280 = 22 mm × 80 mm. mSATA: older, SATA-only, different connector, doesn't fit M.2. CD 700 MB; DVD 4.7 / 8.5 GB; Blu-ray 25 / 50 GB.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A server's memory can find and fix a single flipped bit. Which feature is that, and what does plain parity do instead? (lesson 0048, 3 days ago)

ECC memory detects and corrects single-bit errors. Parity only detects the error; it can't fix it and may halt the system.

An old projector has only VGA. The laptop has HDMI. Is a cheap passive adapter enough, or do you need something else? (lesson 0044, 7 days ago)

You need an active converter: HDMI is digital, VGA is analog, so something has to change the signal. A passive adapter only rewires pins carrying the same signal.

A new PoE camera needs about 20 W, but it keeps rebooting on an older PoE switch. What's the likely mismatch? (lesson 0021, 30 days ago)

The switch only supplies original PoE (802.3af: 15.4 W from the port, about 13 W at the device). The camera needs PoE+ (802.3at: about 25.5 W at the device), from a PoE+ switch or injector.
