Laptop batteries, keyboards and SSDs: the parts you'll actually swap

About 10 minutes. Networking (objectives 2.1–2.8) is done, so today the course moves to mobile devices, starting with laptop hardware.[1] Laptops are cramped and built around their case, so only a few parts are commonly replaced. Today: the battery, the keyboard and the drive. The win: diagnose a dying battery from a report, and plan a hard-drive-to-SSD upgrade without losing anyone's apps.

Why this matters at work

Company laptops are usually encrypted with BitLocker. A planned hardware or firmware change can make the laptop ask for its recovery key at the next boot. Microsoft's guidance: suspend BitLocker before planned work; the drive stays encrypted, and protection resumes on its own after a reboot.[3] If the prompt appears anyway, the recovery key is often backed up to Microsoft Entra ID (or Active Directory), so a technician can look it up instead of the user losing the laptop.[3]

Batteries: lithium-ion, and they wear out

Laptops use two battery types: lithium-ion and lithium-ion polymer.[1] On some laptops the battery is modular: it slides out and a spare slides in. On most thin laptops it's built in, inside the case, and not meant to be swapped by the user.[1]

Every battery loses capacity with age and charge cycles. Windows can show how much:[4]

  • Design capacity: what the battery held when new.
  • Full charge capacity: what it holds today.

Divide the second by the first. A laptop at 40% of its design capacity will run well under half as long as it did new, and no setting will bring that back: the fix is a new battery.

Keyboards and key caps

The keyboard takes the most abuse (spills, crumbs, drops), so it's one of the most often replaced laptop parts.[2] Two sizes of repair:

  • One key cap popped off or broken: the cap clips onto a small hinge underneath, and a replacement cap can be clipped back on. The clips are fragile, so follow the manufacturer's instructions.[2]
  • Whole keyboard (several dead keys, liquid damage): the keyboard connects with a ribbon cable. On some models it lifts out from the top; on others it's built into the palm rest and the laptop has to come apart. Find the service manual first.

Quick workaround while a part is on order: a USB or Bluetooth keyboard.

Hard drive to SSD: the best upgrade a laptop gets

An SSD has no moving parts and reads and writes far faster than a spinning hard drive, so swapping one in makes an old laptop feel new.[5] First, match the form factor and interface the laptop takes:[5][2]

Form factorWhat it looks likeNotes
2.5-inchSmall box, same size as a laptop hard driveA 2.5-inch SATA SSD drops straight into a hard drive's bay. (Desktops use 3.5-inch.)
M.2Bare circuit board, like a stick of gumPlugs into a slot on the motherboard; most modern laptops use it.
1.8-inchSmaller boxOlder, rare; just recognize the name.

Then decide how the data gets there. This is the migration choice:

  • Clone (image) the old drive onto the SSD: Windows, apps, settings and files all come across, and the user carries on as before. With only one drive bay, connect the SSD by a USB-to-SATA adapter or enclosure, clone, then swap.[6]
  • Fresh install on the SSD: a clean start, but apps must be reinstalled and files restored from a backup. In a managed company, this is often the easier path, since the apps and settings come back from device management.

Either way, back up first.[6]

Memory hook

Battery: lithium-ion; full charge ÷ design = health. Keyboard: one cap clips, many keys means a new keyboard. SSD: 2.5-inch or M.2; clone to keep, install to start clean. BitLocker: suspend first.

Try it (2 minutes, laptop only)

Create a battery health report.[4] In a terminal (Command Prompt or PowerShell):

powercfg /batteryreport /output "$env:USERPROFILE\Desktop\battery.html"

(In Command Prompt, use powercfg /batteryreport alone; it saves battery-report.html in the current folder.) Open the file and find the Installed batteries table. Example (yours will differ):

DESIGN CAPACITY        50,000 mWh
FULL CHARGE CAPACITY   41,500 mWh
CYCLE COUNT            312

41,500 ÷ 50,000 = 83% of design capacity. A little wear, normal for a laptop a couple of years old. The report also shows recent usage and estimated battery life, which is useful evidence when someone says "my battery is terrible".

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A two-year-old laptop now lasts about an hour on battery. Its battery report shows design capacity 50,000 mWh and full charge capacity 21,000 mWh. What's the right fix?

21,000 ÷ 50,000 is 42%: the battery now holds well under half its original charge. That's physical wear in the cells, so only a new battery fixes it. Software and drive changes can't add capacity back.

After a laptop was dropped in a bag, one key cap popped off. The small hinge and the key's switch underneath look intact. What's the best fix?

Key caps are replaceable one at a time: a new cap clips onto the intact hinge (carefully, following the manufacturer's instructions). Replacing the whole keyboard is for several dead keys or liquid damage. An external keyboard is a stopgap, not a fix.

A user wants their slow laptop upgraded from a hard drive to an SSD, keeping every app and file exactly as it is. The laptop has one drive bay. What's the plan?

Cloning copies the whole drive, including Windows and installed apps, so nothing needs reinstalling. With one bay, connect the SSD through a USB-to-SATA adapter or enclosure, clone, then swap it in. A fresh install loses the apps; copying one folder loses everything else; there's no room for a second drive.

You're about to do planned hardware work on a BitLocker-encrypted company laptop. What avoids a recovery-key prompt at the next boot?

Suspending leaves the drive fully encrypted but lets it boot after the change; protection resumes on its own after a reboot (unless a reboot count was set). Decrypting is slow and unnecessary. Removing the device from Entra ID would break sign-in and management, and could make the backed-up recovery key harder to find.

From memory: which two numbers in a battery report tell you its health, and what are the two ways to move a user onto a new SSD? (then click to check)

Full charge capacity ÷ design capacity. Migration: clone the old drive (everything comes across) or fresh install (clean start; reinstall apps, restore files). Back up first either way.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A small office's network slows to a crawl whenever someone copies a big file, and the closet has an old hub. Why does a switch fix it, and when would you need a managed switch? (lesson 0020, 3 days ago)

A hub repeats every frame out every port; a switch learns which MAC address is on which port and sends frames only there. You need a managed switch for anything configurable, such as VLANs, traffic prioritization or monitoring.

A remote worker on the company VPN says video calls and web browsing are slow, but only while connected. What's the difference between full tunnel and split tunnel, and which one helps here? (lesson 0016, 7 days ago)

Full tunnel: all traffic goes through the VPN. Split tunnel: only company traffic does; the rest goes straight to the internet. Split tunnel takes the load of calls and browsing off the VPN (a policy decision, since that traffic then bypasses company controls).

Bluetooth: what frequency band, what typical range, and what step must happen before a headset works? (lesson 0009, 14 days ago)

2.4 GHz, about 10 meters, and the devices must be paired first.
