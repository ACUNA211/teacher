How to take it

A user says their three-year-old laptop now lasts under an hour away from the charger. The battery report shows a design capacity of 50,000 mWh and a full charge capacity of 19,000 mWh. What's the right next step?

Battery health = full charge capacity ÷ design capacity: 19,000 ÷ 50,000 = 38%. Lithium-ion wears out with age and charge cycles, and no setting, firmware or calibration brings lost capacity back. The fix is a new battery (built in on most thin laptops, so check the service manual). See lesson 0023.

A user can print to the printer down the hall and open a folder on the local file server, but no website loads. ipconfig /all shows 192.168.1.42, mask 255.255.255.0, a DNS server, and a blank Default Gateway line. What's wrong?

Local works, everything else fails: that's the default gateway's job. Without one, the device has nowhere to send traffic that isn't on its own subnet. It isn't APIPA (that would be 169.254.x.x), local hosts are clearly reachable, and DNS can't help when there's no route out. See lesson 0001.

On a train, a user shares their phone's connection with their laptop over a USB cable. Signing in to work apps now asks for an extra MFA prompt that never appears in the office. What's the most likely reason?

Tethered (one device, by cable) or on a hotspot (many devices, by Wi-Fi), the laptop reaches the internet through the phone, so its sign-ins come from the mobile carrier's public IP, not the office's. A sign-in policy that trusts only office addresses sees "outside" and asks for more proof. That's the policy working, not a fault. See lesson 0025 and lesson 0018.

A user can't use their camera in any app. In Settings → Privacy & security → Camera, the switches are greyed out with "Some settings are managed by your organization." The privacy shutter is open. Where's the fix?

Greyed-out switches plus "managed by your organization" is layer 4 of the order (physical → Windows privacy → device → policy). A setting such as Intune's Allow Camera = Not allowed stops every app while the hardware and driver stay fine, so the fix is in the policy the device receives, not on the laptop. See lesson 0024.

A warehouse wants cheap tags on thousands of boxes, read automatically as pallets roll through the dock door. The tags can't have batteries. Which technology fits?

A passive RFID tag has no battery: the reader's radio signal powers it just long enough to answer, which is why the tags are tiny and cheap. Active RFID needs a battery (ruled out). NFC means a deliberate tap at a few centimeters, not a pallet rolling past, and Bluetooth devices need power and pairing. See lesson 0009.

An employee is leaving. Their personal phone was set up under BYOD for work email and apps. Policy says remove all company data but leave personal photos and apps untouched. Which action do you run?

Retire (selective wipe) takes back the company's apps, data, email profiles and settings and leaves the owner's data alone: exactly BYOD offboarding. Wipe is a factory reset of everything, for company-owned devices that are lost, stolen or being reassigned; on some personal enrollments it isn't even offered. See lesson 0028.

The office installs new Wi-Fi 7 access points, but one laptop's speed doesn't change at all. On that laptop, netsh wlan show drivers lists radio types up to 802.11ac. What explains it?

802.11ac is Wi-Fi 5 (n4, ac5, ax6, be7). Standards are backward compatible, so the Wi-Fi 7 access point still serves the laptop, but a connection runs at the best standard both ends share. The ceiling is the laptop's adapter, not the network. See lesson 0007.

A user's Bluetooth headset sounds great for music. The moment a video meeting starts, the audio turns thin and muffled, and others say the user's voice sounds just as bad. What's the most likely cause?

When a Bluetooth headset's microphone turns on, it switches from its stereo music profile to the Hands-Free Profile, which carries only low-quality mono audio both ways. "Fine on music, terrible in meetings" is that, not a broken headset. Workaround: keep the headset as the speaker and pick the laptop's built-in mic (or a USB headset) in the meeting app. See lesson 0026.

IT tracks company phones in an asset list. A user moves to a new carrier with an eSIM, keeping the same phone. Which identifier in the asset list still matches the device?

The IMEI belongs to the phone itself and survives any SIM or carrier change, which is why it's the one to track assets by. A new carrier means a new SIM profile (new ICCID) and a new subscription (new IMSI), and the number may change too. (The EID, the eSIM chip's ID, also stays, since it's the same phone.) See lesson 0027.

A user signs in to the company portal successfully, MFA and all, but gets "Access denied" when opening the finance app. Which part of AAA is doing its job here?

The sign-in worked, so authentication (who are you?) passed. "Access denied" to one app is authorization (what may you do?): groups, roles and policies. Accounting is the record of it in the sign-in logs. "Auditing" isn't one of the three A's. Next step: check the user's group or role for that app, not their password. See lesson 0010.

A user's laptop has one 2.5-inch drive bay and a slow spinning hard drive. They want an SSD but must keep every app and setting exactly as it is today. What's the plan?

Match the form factor first: a 2.5-inch SATA SSD drops straight into a laptop hard drive's bay (3.5-inch is desktop size, and this laptop has no M.2 slot mentioned). "Keep everything" means clone, not a fresh install; with one bay, connect the SSD by a USB-to-SATA adapter, clone, then swap. Back up first, and suspend BitLocker before the work. See lesson 0023.

Enrolling new laptops in device management fails on the office network with certificate or connection errors. The same laptops enroll fine over a phone hotspot. What's the most likely cause?

"Works at home (or on a hotspot), fails at the office" points at a box in the path. A proxy or security appliance that inspects encrypted traffic breaks the certificate checks that enrollment relies on; Microsoft lists Intune service addresses that should bypass that inspection. A full DHCP scope would mean no address at all (169.254), and the license and Wi-Fi standard don't change between networks. See lesson 0011.

A contractor needs work email on their personal phone but refuses to let IT enroll the device. Security still wants a PIN on the mail app and no copying company data into personal apps. What fits?

MAM protects company data inside work apps (app PIN, no copy-paste or "save as" to personal apps, selective wipe of the app's data) without enrolling the phone. The other three are company-owned models that need full enrollment. The trade-off: without enrollment, IT can't push apps, certificates, Wi-Fi or VPN settings. See lesson 0028.

A laptop has a DHCP reservation so it always gets the same address, which a firewall rule depends on. Undocked on Wi-Fi, it works. On its desk dock with a network cable and Wi-Fi off, it gets a different address and the rule fails. What's the most likely cause?

A DHCP reservation is tied to one MAC address. Docked, the wired network usually comes from a network adapter inside the dock, with its own MAC, so the DHCP server sees a different device and hands out a pool address. Get-NetAdapter docked and undocked shows the two MACs; reserve the one actually used (or both). The dock clearly passes DHCP, since the laptop got an address. See lesson 0026 and lesson 0014.

Marketing starts using a newsletter service. Someone adds a second SPF TXT record for it, next to the existing Microsoft 365 one. Now ordinary staff mail starts failing SPF too. What's the fix?

A domain may have only one SPF record; publishing a second breaks both. Combine them: one v=spf1 record with both include: entries and a single -all at the end. DKIM and DMARC don't repair a broken SPF record, and setting DMARC to none only hides the damage. See lesson 0013.

Explain it in your own words

Say or write the answer before you open each one. If you can explain it to a coworker, you know it.

A coworker asks for "a dock" for a new laptop. Explain the classic difference between a docking station and a port replicator in two sentences. (lesson 0026)

A port replicator just copies the laptop's ports to a box on the desk, is usually smaller, and traditionally doesn't charge the laptop. A docking station copies the ports, adds more (sometimes expansion slots or drives), and charges the laptop through the same connection, often with a vendor-specific connector. Modern USB-C/Thunderbolt docks blur the line.

You're about to update a BitLocker-encrypted laptop's firmware. What do you do first, and what's the plan if it asks for a recovery key anyway? (lesson 0023)

Suspend BitLocker before the planned change: the drive stays encrypted, and protection resumes on its own after a reboot. If the recovery screen appears anyway, look up the recovery key where it's backed up (often Microsoft Entra ID or Active Directory) instead of reinstalling.

A user sees 5G at multi-gigabit speed outdoors downtown, but it drops sharply the moment they walk inside. Explain why, and connect it to what you know about Wi-Fi bands. (lesson 0027, lesson 0006)

That's mmWave 5G: very high frequency, multi-gigabit peaks, but short range and blocked by walls and trees. Indoors the phone falls back to sub-6 GHz 5G (or LTE): wider coverage, lower speed. Same physics as 2.4 vs. 5 GHz Wi-Fi: higher frequency means more speed and less reach.

Explain SPF, DKIM and DMARC to a non-technical manager, using one picture for each. (lesson 0013, 16 days ago)

SPF is the guest list: which servers may send mail for the domain. DKIM is the wax seal: the message was signed by the domain and not changed on the way. DMARC is the bouncer's instructions: what the receiver should do when the list or the seal doesn't check out (none, quarantine, reject), and who gets the reports.

A load balancer and a proxy server both sit "in the middle." Who does each one stand in front of? (lesson 0011, 18 days ago)

A load balancer faces inward: it stands in front of your servers, spreading requests across them behind one address. A proxy faces outward: it stands in for your users, making their requests to the internet (and can cache, filter and log them).
