The four settings every device needs

About 10 minutes. By the end you can look at ipconfig /all and say which missing setting explains a "nothing works" ticket.

Why this matters at work

A user says "I can't sign in to anything." Before you look at their account, check the device. Signing in to a cloud service like Microsoft Entra ID first requires the device to find the sign-in server by name and reach it across the internet. If any of four network settings is wrong, the account can be perfectly fine and sign-in still fails.

The four settings

To talk on an IP network, a device needs four values.[1] Think of them as questions the device must be able to answer:

SettingThe question it answersExample
IP addressWho am I on this network?192.168.1.42
Subnet maskWhich addresses are local, meaning in the same room as me?255.255.255.0
Default gatewayWhere do I send traffic for everything that isn't local?192.168.1.1
DNS serverWho turns names like example.com into IP addresses?192.168.1.1

Coming from programming, you can treat these like a function's required parameters. Leave one out and the call still runs, but only some things work:

  • No gateway: local things work (the printer down the hall), but the internet doesn't.
  • No working DNS: you can reach an IP address directly, but anything typed as a name fails. Since people use names for almost everything, it looks like "the internet is down."

Where the settings come from

Almost nobody types these in by hand. A DHCP server hands all four out automatically when the device joins the network.[1] If the device asks and no DHCP server answers, Windows gives itself a fallback address in the range 169.254.0.0–169.254.255.255. This fallback is called APIPA, a link-local address.[1] It has no gateway and no DNS server, so the device can reach almost nothing.

Rule of thumb

See 169.254.x.x? The problem is the connection to DHCP (cable, Wi-Fi, switch port, or the DHCP server itself), not the user's account.

Reading ipconfig /all

On Windows, ipconfig /all shows every setting for every adapter.[2] Here's a healthy adapter, with the four settings highlighted:

Ethernet adapter Ethernet:

   Description . . . . . . . . . . . : Example Ethernet Controller
   Physical Address. . . . . . . . . : 00-00-5E-00-53-2A
   DHCP Enabled. . . . . . . . . . . : Yes
   IPv4 Address. . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.42(Preferred)
   Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.255.0
   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.1
   DHCP Server . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.1
   DNS Servers . . . . . . . . . . . : 192.168.1.1

And here's one where DHCP never answered:

Ethernet adapter Ethernet:

   DHCP Enabled. . . . . . . . . . . : Yes
   Autoconfiguration IPv4 Address. . : 169.254.83.107(Preferred)
   Subnet Mask . . . . . . . . . . . : 255.255.0.0
   Default Gateway . . . . . . . . . :

The gateway line is blank and the address starts with 169.254. Those two clues together mean APIPA.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. Open a terminal on your own PC and run ipconfig /all.
  2. Find your active adapter and point to each of the four settings.
  3. Check: is DHCP Enabled set to Yes? Is your DNS server the same address as your gateway (common at home) or a different one (common at work)?

Keep the output to yourself. Real addresses from a work network are exactly the kind of detail this public site never stores.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up. Struggling a little is what makes it stick.

A laptop shows 169.254.83.107 and a blank default gateway. What's the most likely cause?

A 169.254.x.x address is APIPA: Windows made up its own address because DHCP never replied. Check the physical or Wi-Fi connection and the DHCP server. A password problem wouldn't change the IP address at all.

A user can open a site by typing its IP address, but typing its name gives "can't find server." Which setting is at fault?

Reaching the IP address proves the IP, mask and gateway all work: traffic is getting out. Only turning names into addresses fails, and that's DNS's job.

A PC can print to a printer on its own local network, but can't reach any internet address, even by IP. What's wrong?

Local traffic works, so the IP address and mask are fine. Traffic leaving the local network goes through the default gateway. If even a raw IP address on the internet fails, the gateway is the suspect, not DNS.

Which setting tells a device whether another address is on its own local network?

The subnet mask marks which part of the address identifies the network. The device compares that part of its own address with the destination's. If they match, the destination is local. If not, traffic goes to the gateway.

Close the page in your head: name all four settings and one symptom of each going wrong. (then click to check)

IP address: nothing works, or a 169.254 address. Subnet mask: local devices look "remote" (or the other way round). Default gateway: local works, internet doesn't. DNS server: IP addresses work, names don't.
