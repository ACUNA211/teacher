Internet connections: six ways to reach the WAN

About 10 minutes. Yesterday's lesson 0017 ended at the WAN. Today is the last stretch: the connection that carries a home or office onto the internet. A+ names six: satellite, fiber, cable, DSL, cellular and WISP.[1] The win: given a site and a symptom, pick the connection type and its weak spot.

Why this matters at work

A user on a phone hotspot (cellular) gets an "unusual sign-in location" alert, or a sign-in log shows a city they've never been to. Microsoft notes that mobile providers hand out IP addresses from central pools that are often far from where the device actually is, so the location shown for an IP is only a best guess.[3] Check how they were connected before you assume a stolen password.

Two numbers: speed and latency

Speed (bandwidth, in Mbps) is how much data flows per second. Latency (in milliseconds) is how long one trip takes. Downloads care about speed; calls, remote desktop and games care about latency. Most home links are asymmetric: download is much faster than upload.[2]

Why satellite latency differs so much

Classic satellite internet uses geostationary satellites about 35,786 km up. Even at the speed of light, the round trip takes nearly half a second, so real-world latency lands around 600 ms. Newer low earth orbit services such as Starlink fly around 550 km up, so latency drops to roughly 25–60 ms,[1][5] close to wired broadband. On the exam, "satellite" still means: remote site, high latency, rain fade.

Memory hooks

Each type has one word: Fiber = fastest. Cable = coax. DSL = distance (phone line, central office). Satellite = delay and rain. Cellular = cap (data plan). WISP = sight (line of sight to the tower).

Try it (2 minutes)

Measure latency yourself. In PowerShell or Command Prompt:

ping -n 4 1.1.1.1

Example output (yours will differ):

Reply from 1.1.1.1: bytes=32 time=14ms TTL=57
Reply from 1.1.1.1: bytes=32 time=12ms TTL=57
...
    Minimum = 12ms, Maximum = 15ms, Average = 13ms

The time= values are round-trip latency. If you can, run it again while connected through your phone's hotspot (cellular) and compare the average. A wired link is often in the teens; cellular is usually higher and jumps around more.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A small field office is miles from any town, with no phone lines, cable or cell signal. Its users mainly need email and web access. Which connection type fits best?

Satellite reaches remote sites where nothing else does: all it needs is a clear view of the sky. DSL needs phone lines (and is distance-limited), cable needs coax, and cellular needs a signal. Email and web browsing tolerate satellite latency well.

A home user on DSL says their speed is far lower than a friend's on the same plan across town. Their equipment checks out. What's the most likely reason?

DSL runs over telephone wire from the provider's central office, and the signal weakens with distance: the farther away, the slower, with a limit of roughly 10,000 feet. Rain fade is satellite, line of sight is WISP, and data caps belong to cellular plans.

Remote desktop sessions from one branch feel sluggish: every click takes about a second to show, yet large downloads finish quickly. Which connection is the branch probably using?

Fast downloads but slow responses means good bandwidth and high latency. That's the signature of geostationary satellite: the signal travels about 36,000 km up and back each way. Interactive apps (remote desktop, calls) suffer; bulk downloads don't.

A rural office gets internet from an antenna on its roof pointed at a provider's tower. Since a neighbor's new barn went up, the connection keeps dropping. What's the cause?

A rooftop antenna aimed at a tower is a WISP (fixed wireless). It needs clear line of sight to the tower; a new building in the path weakens or blocks the signal. Coax is cable, phone-line length is DSL, and data caps are cellular.

From memory: name all six connection types and the one-word weak spot (or strength) of each. (then click to check)

Fiber = fastest (but costly). Cable = coax (shared, slow upload). DSL = distance from the central office. Satellite = delay and rain fade. Cellular = data cap. WISP = line of sight.

What's the difference between a SAN and a NAS? (lesson 0017, yesterday)

A SAN (a network) gives servers block-level storage that acts like a local disk, usually on its own fast, isolated network (Fibre Channel or iSCSI). A NAS (a device) shares files over SMB or NFS.

A load balancer and a proxy both sit in the traffic path. Whom does each one stand in for? (lesson 0011, 7 days ago)

A load balancer guards the servers: it spreads incoming requests across several of them. A proxy speaks for the users: it makes outbound requests on their behalf, so websites see the proxy, not the PC.

POP3 and IMAP both fetch mail. Which ports do they use, and which one keeps mail synced on every device? (lesson 0004, 14 days ago)

POP3: TCP 110, downloads mail to one device. IMAP: TCP 143, keeps mail on the server, synced to every device.
