Same plug, different cable: USB, Thunderbolt and serial

About 10 minutes. The last three lessons were network cables. Today is the cables that connect things to a computer: USB, Thunderbolt and old-school serial.[1] The win: when a USB-C cable "doesn't work", know that the plug tells you almost nothing, and know which label does.

Why this matters at work

A classic ticket: "The new dock charges my laptop but the second monitor stays black." The dock, the laptop and the cable can all have USB-C plugs and still disagree on what they carry. And when you (or the network team) need to reach a switch's console port after a bad config change, the answer is a serial cable, usually through a USB adapter.

1. USB: versions and speeds

USB (Universal Serial Bus) replaced a drawer of older ports. Each version is faster, and newer ports still accept older devices at the older speed.[1][2]

Version (newer name)Top speedTypical max cable
USB 2.0 (Hi-Speed)480 Mbps5 m
USB 3.0 = USB 3.2 Gen 1 (SuperSpeed)5 Gbpsabout 3 m (not fixed by the standard)
USB 3.1 = USB 3.2 Gen 210 Gbpsshorter; often 1 m or less for passive cables
USB 3.2 Gen 2x220 Gbps (two 10 Gbps lanes)short, USB-C only

Yes, the names were renamed twice, so "USB 3.0" and "USB 3.2 Gen 1" are the same 5 Gbps. Read the speed on the box, not the version number.[2][3] Cable lengths are typical exam-style figures; the faster the link, the shorter a passive cable can be.

2. USB-C is a shape, not a speed

USB-C is the small, reversible 24-pin connector you met in lesson 0025. It describes the plug. What travels through it depends on the port and the cable: USB 2.0 at 480 Mbps, USB 3.2 at 5–20 Gbps, video, power, or Thunderbolt.[1] A cheap USB-C charging cable may carry only USB 2.0 data. Same shape, very different cable.

3. Thunderbolt

Thunderbolt is a high-speed serial link from Intel (with Apple) that carries data, video and power on one cable, and can daisy-chain devices.[1]

VersionConnectorSpeed
Thunderbolt 1Mini DisplayPort10 Gbps (two 10 Gbps channels)
Thunderbolt 2Mini DisplayPort20 Gbps (channels combined)
Thunderbolt 3 and 4USB-C40 Gbps
Thunderbolt 5USB-C80 Gbps (up to 120 Gbps one way)

Professor Messer's exam figure for copper Thunderbolt cable is a maximum of about 3 m; full 40 Gbps over a passive cable needs a much shorter one, and longer runs use active or optical cables.[1][4] Thunderbolt 4 also sets minimums a USB-C port doesn't have to meet: 40 Gbps, two 4K displays and 15 W for accessories.[5] Look for the lightning-bolt icon next to the port.

Back to the dock ticket: a Thunderbolt dock plugged into a plain USB-C port (or through a USB 2.0-only cable) often charges but can't drive both monitors. Check the laptop's port icon and the cable's rating before blaming drivers.

4. Serial: RS-232 and DB-9

Before USB, devices talked over serial cables using the RS-232 standard, on a DB-9 (9-pin) or older DB-25 connector.[1] It's slow, but it isn't dead: switches, routers, firewalls and industrial gear still have a console port for setup and recovery, reached with a serial cable even when the network is down. Modern laptops have no DB-9, so you use a USB-to-serial adapter, which shows up in Windows as a COM port.[1]

Memory hook

USB 2.0 = 480 Mbps, 5 m. USB 3.x Gen 1 / 2 / 2x2 = 5 / 10 / 20 Gbps. USB-C is the plug, not the speed. Thunderbolt: 1–2 on Mini DisplayPort (10, 20), 3–4 on USB-C (40), bolt icon. Serial = RS-232 on DB-9, for console ports, via USB adapter.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. Look at the USB-C ports on your laptop. Is there a lightning bolt (Thunderbolt), a "SS" with a number (USB SuperSpeed 5/10/20), or no icon at all?
  2. In PowerShell, list what's connected over USB and any serial ports: 
    Get-PnpDevice -PresentOnly -Class USB | Format-Table Status, FriendlyName
Get-PnpDevice -PresentOnly -Class Ports | Format-Table Status, FriendlyName
    Host controllers named "USB 3.x eXtensible" are your SuperSpeed ports. If you plug in a USB-to-serial adapter, it appears under Ports as "… (COM3)" or similar: that COM number is what a terminal program needs.[6]
A user copies a 20 GB video to a new "USB 3.2" external SSD and it crawls at about 40 MB/s. The drive's own cable is USB-C to USB-A, plugged into a black port on an old desktop. What's your first guess? (then click to check)

The port is probably USB 2.0 (480 Mbps ≈ 40–50 MB/s in practice). USB is backward compatible, so the drive works, but only at the older port's speed. Move it to a USB 3 port (often blue or marked "SS") and the copy should be many times faster.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A laptop and a dock both have USB-C, but the dock's two monitors stay dark while charging works. The dock is labeled Thunderbolt 4. What's the most likely cause?

USB-C is only the connector. A Thunderbolt dock on a plain USB-C port (no bolt icon), or through a low-speed cable, often gets power but not the bandwidth for two displays. Check the port icon and the cable rating.

Which pairing of USB version and top speed is correct?

USB 3.2 Gen 1 is the renamed USB 3.0: 5 Gbps. USB 2.0 is 480 Mbps, Gen 2 is 10 Gbps, Gen 2x2 is 20 Gbps.

A switch was misconfigured and dropped off the network. You need to reach its command line directly. What do you connect?

Network gear has a console port that speaks RS-232 serial, independent of the network. Laptops lack DB-9 ports, so a USB-to-serial adapter (a COM port in Windows) fills the gap.

An older Mac has a Thunderbolt port shaped like Mini DisplayPort. What is its fastest possible version and speed?

Thunderbolt 1 and 2 used the Mini DisplayPort connector (10 and 20 Gbps). From Thunderbolt 3 on, the connector is USB-C.

From memory: the four USB speeds, what USB-C does and doesn't tell you, the Thunderbolt connectors and speeds, and what serial is still used for. (then click to check)

USB: 2.0 = 480 Mbps; 3.2 Gen 1 (3.0) = 5 Gbps; Gen 2 = 10 Gbps; Gen 2x2 = 20 Gbps. USB-C: a reversible 24-pin plug; says nothing about speed. Thunderbolt: 1/2 on Mini DisplayPort (10/20 Gbps), 3/4 on USB-C (40 Gbps), 5 at 80 Gbps; about 3 m copper. Serial: RS-232 on DB-9, for console ports on network and industrial gear, via a USB-to-serial adapter.

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A contractor wants to run ordinary PVC-jacketed Ethernet above the drop ceiling, where the building's air returns flow. What's the problem, and what should the jacket say instead? (lesson 0037, 3 days ago)

That space is a plenum: in a fire, burning PVC gives off toxic smoke that the air system spreads. Use plenum-rated cable, printed CMP (CMR is only for risers).

A laptop dock adds monitors, USB ports and Ethernet, plus has its own power supply and extra hardware. A cheaper box only copies the laptop's existing ports. Which is the docking station and which is the port replicator? (lesson 0026, 14 days ago)

The first is a docking station (adds capabilities). The second is a port replicator (just extends the ports the laptop already has). Today's twist: either may need Thunderbolt to drive several displays.

The firewall, switches and servers all send their logs to one box over UDP 514. What's that service, and which severity number is the worst? (lesson 0010, 30 days ago)

A syslog server. Severities run from 0 (emergency), the worst, to 7 (debug). The logs often feed a SIEM.
