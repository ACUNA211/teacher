Light instead of electricity: optical fiber

About 10 minutes. The last two lessons were copper: twisted pairs and their color order. Today the signal is light in a thin strand of glass. The win: say when fiber is the right cable, and tell single-mode from multimode by what it does, how far it goes and (usually) the color of its jacket.[1]

Why this matters at work

You'll rarely run fiber to a desk, but you'll meet it: the uplink between two network closets, the link between buildings, and the ISP's fiber that ends at an ONT (lesson 0021). When "the whole floor is offline" while one floor up is fine, a bent or unplugged fiber uplink is a real suspect, long before anyone's account or laptop.

1. Why use light?

Copper carries electrical signals, which pick up interference and fade over distance (Cat 6A tops out at 100 m, lesson 0037). Fiber carries pulses of light instead.[1]

Fiber's strengthsFiber's costs
No electrical signal, so it's immune to radio and electrical interference (motors, power lines)Usually more expensive than copper, cable and the optics at each end
The signal fades slowly: runs of kilometers, not 100 mHarder to install and terminate; you can't crimp it like RJ45
Hard to tap: nothing radiates from the cable to eavesdrop onFragile: bend it too tightly and the glass breaks inside the jacket

"Hard to tap" isn't "impossible": bending a fiber can leak some light out to a listener. It's just far harder than with copper.[3]

2. Single-mode vs. multimode

The glass has a core that carries the light. Its width sets the type.[1]

Multimode (MMF)Single-mode (SMF)
CoreWider: light bounces along many paths ("modes")Much narrower: one path
DistanceShort range, up to about 2 kmLong range, up to about 100 km without regenerating the signal
Light sourceInexpensive, often LEDsExpensive lasers
Typical useInside a building or a campus: closet to closet, data center racksBetween sites, ISP and telecom links
Usual jacket colorOrange (older) or aquaYellow

Distances and light sources follow Professor Messer's A+ fiber video;[1][2] real reach depends on the speed and the optics, so treat 2 km and 100 km as exam numbers, not guarantees. Why multimode is short: light taking many paths arrives at slightly different times and smears over distance. One path stays sharp much longer.

Jacket colors are a convention (orange = OM1/OM2, aqua = OM3/OM4, yellow = single-mode), not a rule. Read the printing on the jacket ("SM OS2", "MM OM3") to be sure.[4] And you can't mix them: a single-mode optic on a multimode cable won't give a reliable link.

3. Handle it safely

  • Never look into the end of a fiber or an optic port. The laser light can be invisible and can damage your eyes.[5]
  • Don't bend it tightly. Bends smaller than a few inches across can damage the fiber and cause problems that are hard to diagnose.[5] Don't pinch it in a door or zip-tie it hard.
  • Keep the dust caps on unplugged ends; a dirty connector end dims the light.[5]
Memory hook

Fiber = light: no interference, long distance, hard to tap, but pricier and fragile. Multi = many paths = short (≈2 km), cheap LEDs, orange/aqua. Single = one path = long (≈100 km), lasers, yellow. Never look into the end.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. Next time you're near a network rack or a switch with an uplink, look for thin cables that don't end in RJ45. Note the jacket color: yellow, orange or aqua?
  2. Read the jacket printing if you can reach it (for example "OM3 50/125" or "OS2 9/125"). The numbers are the core/cladding size in microns: the tiny 9 µm core is single-mode.
A factory wants to link an office to a warehouse 400 m away. The cable path runs past large electric motors. Copper or fiber, and which kind? (then click to check)

Fiber. 400 m is far beyond copper's 100 m limit, and fiber ignores the motors' interference. Multimode covers 400 m (it's under about 2 km) with cheaper optics; single-mode would also work but costs more than needed.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

Two buildings on a campus sit 300 m apart, and the cable must cross a noisy plant floor. Which cable fits best?

Twisted pair, even Cat 6A, stops at 100 m. Multimode fiber covers short ranges (up to about 2 km) and, carrying light, isn't affected by the plant's electrical noise.

An ISP links two cities 60 km apart with fiber. Which type do they use, with which light source?

Single-mode has a narrow core with one light path, so the signal stays sharp for long distances (up to about 100 km). It uses lasers. Multimode, with cheaper LEDs, is for short runs.

A security team prefers fiber for a sensitive link between two server rooms. What property of fiber are they after?

Fiber carries light, not an electrical signal, so nothing radiates from the cable and it's very hard to tap. It doesn't encrypt anything by itself, and it usually costs more than copper.

After a closet was tidied, a fiber uplink drops errors and flaps. The cable is plugged in at both ends. What's the likely cause?

Fiber ignores electrical interference and has no T568 colors or shield to ground. But it's fragile: a tight bend or a hard zip tie can crack the glass or leak light, causing hard-to-diagnose errors. Ease the bend, then test or replace the cable.

From memory: three reasons to choose fiber over copper, two downsides, and the single-mode vs. multimode differences (core, distance, light source, jacket color). (then click to check)

Pros: immune to interference (no electrical signal), long distances, hard to tap. Cons: more expensive, harder to install, fragile. Multimode: wider core, short range (≈2 km), LEDs, orange/aqua. Single-mode: narrow core, long range (≈100 km), lasers, yellow.

Review: earlier lessons

A cable reads white-green, green, white-orange… on one end and white-orange, orange, white-green… on the other. Which standards are these, what kind of cable is it, and why does it usually still work between two switches? (lesson 0038, 1 day ago)

White-green first is T568A; white-orange first is T568B. A on one end and B on the other is a crossover cable. It works switch to switch anyway (as would straight-through) because of Auto-MDIX.

Your company uses Microsoft 365 for email. Is that IaaS, PaaS or SaaS? And in that model, name one thing that's still the customer's job. (lesson 0032, 7 days ago)

SaaS: the provider runs the whole application. The customer still owns its data, accounts and access management (who gets in, MFA, permissions) and its endpoints.

Which wireless technology fits each: a badge reader at a door, paying with a phone at a till, and wireless earbuds? (lesson 0009, 30 days ago)

Badge reader: RFID (passive tags powered by the reader). Phone payment: NFC (a few centimeters, a tap). Earbuds: Bluetooth (2.4 GHz, about 10 m).
