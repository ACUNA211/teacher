When the plug doesn't fit: adapters and converters

About 10 minutes. Last week you met the cables. In real life you rarely have exactly the right one, but you might have an adapter or converter that bridges the gap.[1] The win: look at two ports and know whether a cheap passive adapter will do, or whether you need something that actively changes the signal.

Why this matters at work

Thin laptops have no Ethernet port, so users plug in a USB-to-Ethernet adapter or a dock. That adapter is its own network card with its own MAC address. Anything keyed to a MAC (a DHCP reservation, a MAC allowlist on the network) follows the adapter, not the laptop: swap adapters and the "known device" suddenly looks new.

1. Adapter or converter?

Adapter (passive)Converter (active)
What it doesRewires one connector shape to another. The signal passes through unchanged.Has a chip that changes the signal, e.g. digital to analog.
Works whenBoth sides already speak the same signal.The two sides speak different signals.
PowerNone needed.[2]Often draws power (USB or its own supply).

So the question is never "does it fit?" but "is the same signal on both sides?"

2. Video: DVI to HDMI, DVI to VGA

Recall from lesson 0041: DVI-D is digital only, DVI-A analog only, DVI-I both; HDMI is digital; VGA is analog.

  • DVI ↔ HDMI: passive adapter works. Both use the same digital video signaling (TMDS). Catch: DVI carries no audio, so sound has to travel another way.[3]
  • DVI-I → VGA: passive adapter works. DVI-I has analog pins, and the adapter just routes them to the VGA plug.[2]
  • DVI-D → VGA: needs an active converter. DVI-D has no analog pins at all, so a passive dongle has nothing to pass on.[2][4] Same rule for HDMI or DisplayPort to VGA: digital to analog always takes a converter.

3. USB: Ethernet, USB-C to USB-A, and hubs

  • USB to Ethernet: an RJ45 port on a stick. Windows sees a new network adapter with its own MAC. Its speed is capped by the USB port: USB 2.0 tops out at 480 Mbps (lesson 0040), so a gigabit adapter needs a USB 3.x port to reach gigabit.
  • USB-C to USB-A: lets an older USB-A device (flash drive, receiver) plug into a USB-C-only laptop. The link runs at the slower side's USB version.
  • USB hub: turns one port into several, all sharing that port's bandwidth and power.[1]
    • Bus-powered (no power brick): all ports share what one host port gives, about 500 mA on USB 2.0 or 900 mA on USB 3.x. Fine for a keyboard and mouse.[5]
    • Self-powered (own power supply): can give each port its full allowance. Use it for external drives and other hungry devices.[5]
Memory hook

Same signal → passive adapter. Different signal → active converter. DVI↔HDMI passive (no audio). DVI-I→VGA passive, DVI-D→VGA converter. USB-Ethernet = new NIC, new MAC. Hungry devices → self-powered hub.

  1. In PowerShell, list your network adapters and their MAC addresses: 
    Get-NetAdapter | Format-Table Name, InterfaceDescription, Status, LinkSpeed, MacAddress
    A USB or dock Ethernet adapter usually has "USB" in its InterfaceDescription. If you have one, plug it in and run the command again: a new row appears with a different MAC from the built-in Wi-Fi card.
A projector has only a VGA input. The laptop has only HDMI. Will a cheap passive HDMI-to-VGA plug work? (then click to check)

No. HDMI is digital and VGA is analog, so the signal must be changed. You need an active converter (the ones sold as "HDMI to VGA" have a chip inside, often with USB power).

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A graphics card has only a DVI-D port. The monitor has only VGA. A passive DVI-to-VGA dongle gives a black screen. Why?

A passive adapter only reroutes pins. DVI-D is digital only, so there are no analog pins to route to VGA. It needs an active converter. A DVI-I port would have worked with the passive dongle.

A PC connects to a TV with a passive DVI-to-HDMI adapter. The picture is perfect, but the TV speakers are silent. What's the most likely reason?

DVI and HDMI share the same digital video signal, so the picture passes through. But DVI has no audio, so sound needs another path (a separate audio cable, or a direct HDMI connection).

A user plugs a portable hard drive into a small USB hub that has no power supply. The drive clicks and keeps disconnecting. What's the best fix?

A bus-powered hub shares one port's power (about 500 mA on USB 2.0, 900 mA on USB 3.x) across all its devices. A hard drive can need more. A self-powered hub has its own supply and can feed each port fully.

A thin laptop uses a gigabit USB-to-Ethernet adapter, but the link only reaches about 300 Mbps in tests. The adapter is in a USB 2.0 port. What's the cause?

The adapter can't go faster than the port it's in. USB 2.0 is 480 Mbps on paper and less in practice. Move it to a USB 3.x port (5 Gbps or more) to reach gigabit.

From memory: the difference between an adapter and a converter, the three DVI cases (to HDMI, DVI-I to VGA, DVI-D to VGA), and the two kinds of USB hub. (then click to check)

Adapter: passive, same signal, new shape. Converter: active chip, changes the signal. DVI→HDMI passive (no audio); DVI-I→VGA passive (analog pins); DVI-D→VGA active converter. Hubs: bus-powered (share one port's power) and self-powered (own supply, full power per port).

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

Of VGA, DVI-D, HDMI and DisplayPort, which one is analog only, and which two also carry audio? (lesson 0041, 3 days ago)

VGA is analog only (and video only). HDMI and DisplayPort carry digital video and audio. DVI-D is digital video with no audio.

A cable has to run through the air-handling space above a drop ceiling. What kind of jacket is required, and why? (lesson 0037, 7 days ago)

Plenum-rated cable (CMP). Its fire-retardant jacket (e.g. FEP or low-smoke PVC) gives off less smoke and fumes in a fire than ordinary PVC, which matters where air is circulated through the building. Trade-off: it's stiffer and costs more.

Name two reasons from the A+ objectives to run a virtual machine, and what happens to your files when you close Windows Sandbox. (lesson 0030, 14 days ago)

Any two of: sandboxing, developing/testing software, legacy software, cross-platform. Windows Sandbox discards everything when it closes: nothing persists.
