Bluetooth, NFC, RFID and fixed wireless

About 10 minutes. The last two lessons were Wi-Fi. Today: the four other wireless technologies in the same exam objective. The trick is to sort them by range: kilometers, meters, a few meters, a few centimeters. Range tells you what each one is for.

Why this matters at work

Passwordless sign-in leans on these radios. A FIDO2 security key, which Microsoft Entra ID accepts as a sign-in method, can talk to the device over USB, NFC or Bluetooth Low Energy.[5] When "my key won't work" turns out to be "the phone's NFC is off" or "Bluetooth isn't paired", you've fixed a sign-in problem without touching the account.

Four technologies, longest range first

1. Long-range fixed wireless (kilometers)

A radio link between two fixed places, such as two buildings or a tower and a building, using a directional antenna at each end that aims a narrow beam at the other.[2] Used to join a second building without trenching cable, or to bring internet to places without wired service. Exam points:[1]

  • Licensed or unlicensed spectrum. Licensed: you pay for exclusive use of a frequency, so no one else interferes. Unlicensed: free (often the same 2.4 and 5 GHz bands as Wi-Fi) but shared.
  • Power is regulated. Regulators (the FCC in the US) cap how strongly you may transmit, and the cap depends on band and antenna gain.[3] You can't simply turn the power all the way up.

2. Bluetooth (about 10 meters)

A personal area network (PAN) for headsets, mice, keyboards, speakers and phones. It uses the 2.4 GHz band (the same one as lesson 0006's crowded Wi-Fi channels), and consumer devices reach about 10 meters.[1] Devices must be paired once before they connect, and they need to be in pairing mode, in range and charged.[6]

3. RFID (centimeters to meters)

Radio-frequency identification: a small tag answers a reader. Access badges, inventory and assembly-line tracking, pet ID chips.[1] The surprising part: a passive tag has no battery. The reader's radio signal powers the tag just long enough for it to reply. That's why passive range is short and the tags are cheap and tiny. Active tags carry a battery and reach much farther.[4]

4. NFC (about 4 centimeters)

Near field communication grew out of RFID and runs at 13.56 MHz, but it's two-way and works only at a few centimeters.[7] That tiny range is the point: you have to mean to tap. Tap-to-pay, tapping a phone to a badge reader, tapping a security key to a phone.

Try it (2 minutes)

On a Windows laptop, open PowerShell and run:

Get-PnpDevice -Class Bluetooth

You'll see the Bluetooth radio itself plus every paired device, each with a Status:

Status  Class      FriendlyName
------  -----      ------------
OK      Bluetooth  Intel(R) Wireless Bluetooth(R)
OK      Bluetooth  Example Headset
Unknown Bluetooth  Example Mouse

Your list will differ. OK = present and working. Unknown usually means paired but not connected right now (off, asleep or out of range). Error on the radio line points to a driver or hardware problem, not the headset. The everyday fix path is Settings → Bluetooth & devices: remove the device, put it in pairing mode, add it again.[6]

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

Employees open the office door by holding a thin plastic badge near a reader. The badge has no battery. What technology is most likely?

A passive RFID tag has no battery: the reader's radio signal powers it long enough to reply with its ID. Bluetooth and Wi-Fi both need a powered radio in the badge. (Some newer badges use NFC, which is built on the same idea.)

A company leases a second building across a wide parking lot and wants it on the same network, without digging a trench for cable. What fits best?

Long-range fixed wireless links two fixed sites with a directional antenna at each end, aimed at each other. Bluetooth reaches about 10 meters, NFC a few centimeters, and RFID only identifies tags; none of them carry a building's network traffic.

A user's wireless headset works at their desk but cuts out when they walk to the break room about 25 meters away, leaving the laptop behind. Most likely cause?

Consumer Bluetooth reaches about 10 meters, so 25 meters away from the laptop is out of range. A headset paired to a laptop doesn't get a DHCP address or use the Wi-Fi access point, and it isn't an NFC device.

To approve a sign-in, a user holds a hardware security key against the back of their phone for a second. Which technology carries that tap?

Holding two things together for a second is NFC's signature: two-way, a few centimeters, tap to use. Security keys accepted by Microsoft Entra ID can connect over USB, NFC or Bluetooth Low Energy.

From memory: an installer wants to turn a fixed wireless link's power all the way up "for a stronger signal." What do you tell them, and what's the difference between licensed and unlicensed spectrum? (then click to check)

Transmit power is capped by regulators (the FCC in the US), depending on the band and antenna. Licensed spectrum is paid-for, exclusive use with no one else interfering; unlicensed is free but shared with everyone else nearby.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A spec sheet says "Wi-Fi 5." Which 802.11 standard, which band, and can an 802.11g-only device join that network? (lesson 0007, 2 days ago)

802.11ac, 5 GHz only. An 802.11g device is 2.4 GHz only, so it can't see a 5 GHz-only network: backward compatibility only works within a band.

Which three 2.4 GHz channels don't overlap, and why does that matter for Bluetooth too? (lesson 0006, 3 days ago)

Channels 1, 6 and 11. Bluetooth also lives in 2.4 GHz, so a crowded 2.4 GHz office is noisy for both Wi-Fi and Bluetooth devices.

Loading a web page uses TCP, but a DNS lookup normally uses UDP. Why the difference? (lesson 0002, 7 days ago)

A web page must arrive complete and in order, so it uses TCP's reliable, connection-oriented delivery. A DNS lookup is one small question and answer: UDP skips the connection setup, and if the reply is lost the client just asks again.
