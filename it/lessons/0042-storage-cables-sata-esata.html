Inside the case and just outside it: SATA and eSATA

About 10 minutes. This week's cables have carried network data, peripherals and video. Today's carry storage: the SATA cables that connect drives inside a desktop, and their external cousin, eSATA.[1] The win: know the two plugs every SATA drive needs, the speed and length limits, and why eSATA isn't "just SATA on the outside".

Why this matters at work

Desktop refreshes and "the PC doesn't see the second drive" tickets. Before blaming the drive or the operating system, check the two cables: a data cable that's loose at the motherboard, or a power plug that never got connected, looks exactly like a dead disk.

1. Internal SATA: two cables per drive

A 2.5- or 3.5-inch SATA drive (hard drive, SATA SSD, optical drive) has two separate connectors side by side:

ConnectorPinsGoes toCarries
SATA data7 (two signal pairs + three grounds)A SATA port on the motherboardData, one drive per cable
SATA power15 (wider plug)The power supply3.3 V, 5 V and 12 V

Both plugs are L-shaped (keyed) so they only go in one way.[2] The 15-pin power plug replaced the old 4-pin Molex; its extra pins give each voltage several contacts and extra grounds for hot-plugging.[2] An internal SATA data cable is limited to about 1 meter.[3]

Speeds come from the SATA revision, and every revision is backward compatible:

  • SATA 1.0: 1.5 Gbps
  • SATA 2.0: 3 Gbps
  • SATA 3.0: 6 Gbps (about 600 MB/s of real data), today's normal[2]

That 6 Gbps ceiling is why fast SSDs moved to M.2 NVMe, which plugs straight into the board with no cable at all (lesson 0023).

2. eSATA: SATA for the outside

eSATA (external SATA) connects an external drive at SATA speed. It isn't just the internal cable through a hole in the case:[1][4]

  • Different plug: no L-shaped key, made to survive many more plug-ins. A SATA cable doesn't fit an eSATA port, and the reverse: each needs its own cable.[1]
  • Shielded cable with stronger signal levels, so it reaches about 2 meters instead of 1.[4]
  • No power: the drive needs its own power adapter. (A combined eSATAp port adds USB-style power, but it was never common.)[2]

Today eSATA is mostly legacy: USB 3.x, USB-C and Thunderbolt (lesson 0040) carry data and power on one cable, so new external drives use those.

Memory hook

SATA drive = two plugs: 7-pin data to the board, 15-pin power to the PSU. 1.5 / 3 / 6 Gbps. Internal 1 m, eSATA 2 m. eSATA: shielded, different plug, no power.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. In PowerShell, list the physical drives and how each one is attached: 
    Get-PhysicalDisk | Format-Table FriendlyName, BusType, MediaType, Size
    BusType shows SATA for a SATA drive, NVMe for an M.2 NVMe SSD and USB for an external drive in a USB enclosure; MediaType says SSD or HDD. A drive missing here (and in Disk Management) points at a cable, power or BIOS setting, not at Windows.
A technician adds a second SATA hard drive to a desktop and connects the data cable to the motherboard. The drive doesn't spin and doesn't appear anywhere. What was most likely missed? (then click to check)

The 15-pin SATA power connector from the power supply. A SATA drive needs both cables: data to the board, power to the PSU. No power means no spin and no detection.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

How many pins does the connector that carries power to an internal SATA drive have?

SATA power is the wide 15-pin plug (3.3, 5 and 12 V). The narrow 7-pin plug is SATA data; 4-pin is old Molex; 19 pins is HDMI.

A user wants an external drive two meters from the PC at SATA speed. The drive has an eSATA port. What should the technician use?

eSATA uses its own shielded cable and plug, rated to about 2 m. It carries no power, so the drive needs its own adapter. Internal SATA cables are only rated to about 1 m and don't fit eSATA ports.

A new SATA 3.0 SSD is installed on an older motherboard with SATA 2.0 ports. What happens?

SATA is backward compatible: the link runs at the slower side's speed, here SATA 2.0's 3 Gbps.

A fast SSD tops out near 550 MB/s in benchmarks. A colleague says the drive is faulty. What's the likely explanation?

SATA 3.0 runs at 6 Gbps, about 600 MB/s of real data, so ~550 MB/s is a healthy SATA SSD. Faster speeds need an M.2 NVMe SSD.

From memory: the two connectors on a SATA drive (pins, where they go), the three SATA speeds, and three ways eSATA differs from internal SATA. (then click to check)

7-pin data to the motherboard, 15-pin power (3.3/5/12 V) to the PSU. 1.5, 3, 6 Gbps (SATA 1.0/2.0/3.0). eSATA: different, unkeyed plug; shielded, up to 2 m (internal 1 m); no power (eSATAp adds it).

A campus link must run 10 km between buildings on one fiber run. Single-mode or multimode, and what color is the jacket usually? (lesson 0039, 3 days ago)

Single-mode: narrow core, laser light, many kilometers without regenerating. Usually a yellow jacket. Multimode (orange or aqua) is for shorter runs, up to about 2 km.

Employees use their own phones for work email. IT wants to protect company data in work apps without managing the whole phone. Which ownership model is this, and what kind of Intune policy fits? (lesson 0028, 14 days ago)

BYOD (bring your own device). App protection policies (MAM) protect data inside the work apps, or a personally owned work profile partitions work from personal. COPE is the company-owned model.

Which DNS record type points a name at another name instead of an IP address, and which one tells the world where to deliver a domain's email? (lesson 0012, 30 days ago)

CNAME points a name at another name (an alias). MX names the mail servers; the lowest preference number is tried first.
