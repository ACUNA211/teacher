From analog to all-in-one: VGA, DVI, HDMI, DisplayPort and USB-C

About 10 minutes. Yesterday's cables carried data. Today's carry the picture: five ways to get video from a computer to a screen.[1] The win: tell the connectors apart on sight, and know which ones carry sound and which adapters will actually work.

Why this matters at work

Conference-room and hot-desk tickets: "the projector says no signal", "the monitor works but there's no sound", "I need an adapter for the old screen". Most of them come down to which signal the port sends (analog or digital, video only or video plus audio) and whether the adapter in the drawer can convert it.

Messer's 1201 video covers exactly these five.[1] VGA is the legacy one: analog, video only. Because the signal is analog, long or poor cables give a softer, ghosting picture rather than simply cutting out.[2]

2. DVI: three types, two link widths

  • DVI-A: analog only (basically VGA on a DVI plug).
  • DVI-D: digital only.
  • DVI-I: integrated, both digital and analog.[1]

DVI-D and DVI-I come in single link and dual link. Dual link adds a second set of pins (a fuller pin grid) for higher resolutions: single link is commonly rated to about 1920 × 1200, dual link to about 2560 × 1600.[1][3] Today DVI is mostly found on older monitors and gear.[4]

3. HDMI and DisplayPort: video plus audio

Both are digital and carry video and audio on one cable.[1] HDMI is the standard on TVs and projectors; DisplayPort is common on business monitors and docks. They're not plug-compatible, but adapters bridge them:

  • DisplayPort → HDMI or DVI: a cheap passive adapter usually works, because the DisplayPort source can switch to sending a compatible signal.[1]
  • HDMI ↔ DVI-D: passive works too (same digital video), but DVI drops the audio.[3]
  • Digital → VGA: needs an active converter (a chip that turns digital into analog). A plain wire can't do it.

4. Video over USB-C

A USB-C port can carry video using DisplayPort Alternate Mode: some of the cable's high-speed lanes stop carrying USB data and carry DisplayPort instead.[5] That's why a USB-C-to-HDMI adapter works on many laptops (DisplayPort inside, converted to HDMI), and why a USB-C port with no Alt Mode, or a USB 2.0-only cable, gives no picture at all. Thunderbolt ports always support video.

Memory hook

VGA = analog, video only, blue, 15 pins in 3 rows. DVI = video only; A analog, D digital, I both; dual link = more pixels. HDMI and DisplayPort = digital video + audio. DP → HDMI/DVI passive; anything digital → VGA needs active. USB-C video = DisplayPort Alt Mode.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. Look at the back of your monitor and the side of your laptop. Name every video port you see.
  2. In PowerShell, ask Windows how each screen is connected: 
    Get-CimInstance -Namespace root\wmi -ClassName WmiMonitorConnectionParams |
  Format-Table InstanceName, VideoOutputTechnology
    Common values: 0 = VGA, 4 = DVI, 5 = HDMI, 10 = DisplayPort (external), 11 = DisplayPort embedded (a laptop's own panel), 2147483648 = internal.[6] A monitor on a USB-C dock usually shows 10, because the video is DisplayPort inside. Some drivers report odd values, so treat it as a hint.
A meeting room's projector has only VGA. A user's new laptop has only HDMI and USB-C. They bring a simple HDMI-to-VGA cable with no box or chip, and get no picture. Why? (then click to check)

HDMI is digital and VGA is analog. A plain cable can't convert between them; you need an active converter (a small box or dongle with a chip, sometimes with its own power). And VGA carries no audio, so sound has to come from the laptop or a separate cable.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user connects a laptop to a monitor with a DVI-D cable through an HDMI adapter. The picture is perfect, but the monitor speakers are silent. Why?

HDMI and DVI-D share the same digital video, so a passive adapter gives a picture. But DVI carries no audio. Use HDMI or DisplayPort end to end, or send audio another way.

Which video connector sends an analog signal and uses fifteen pins in three rows?

VGA: analog, video only, the blue DE-15 (often called DB-15) connector with three rows of pins. HDMI, DisplayPort and DVI-D are all digital.

A dock's DisplayPort output must drive a monitor that has only HDMI. What's the simplest fix that usually works?

A DisplayPort source can send an HDMI-compatible signal, so a passive DisplayPort-to-HDMI adapter usually works. Active converters are for digital-to-analog (VGA).

A laptop's USB-C port charges a phone and reads flash drives, but a USB-C-to-HDMI adapter gives no picture. What's most likely missing?

USB-C carries video only if the port supports DisplayPort Alt Mode (or Thunderbolt). A data-and-charging-only USB-C port can't output video, whatever the adapter.

From memory: for each of VGA, DVI, HDMI and DisplayPort, is it analog or digital, and does it carry audio? What do the DVI letters mean? (then click to check)

VGA: analog, video only. DVI: video only; DVI-A analog, DVI-D digital, DVI-I both; single vs. dual link (dual = higher resolution). HDMI and DisplayPort: digital, video + audio. USB-C: video via DisplayPort Alt Mode. Passive DP → HDMI/DVI works; digital → VGA needs an active converter.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

You crimp one end of a patch cable as T568A and the other as T568B. What kind of cable did you make, and why might it still work on modern gear? (lesson 0038, 3 days ago)

A crossover cable (orange and green pairs swapped: 1↔3, 2↔6). Modern ports with Auto-MDIX detect it and adjust, so it often works anyway. For normal patch cables, use the same standard (usually T568B) on both ends.

A display panel needs no backlight, has perfect blacks, but can suffer burn-in from static images. Which display type is it? (lesson 0034, 7 days ago)

OLED: each pixel makes its own light. LCDs (including "LED" and Mini-LED screens) need a backlight.

Users reach one web address, but behind it several servers share the work, and a failed server stops getting traffic. Which appliance does that? (lesson 0011, 30 days ago)

A load balancer. It faces inward, protecting and scaling your servers, unlike a proxy, which sits in front of your users.
