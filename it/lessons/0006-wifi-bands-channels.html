Wi-Fi bands and channels

About 10 minutes. Ports are done; now the radio underneath. Wi-Fi talks on three frequency bands, each cut into channels. Pick the wrong band and the laptop is slow; pick overlapping channels and two access points shout over each other.

Why this matters at work

"Wi-Fi is fine at my desk but crawls in the far meeting room" is usually a band problem, not an account problem. Knowing which band a laptop picked (and why) lets you explain it instead of rebooting things. Later, when you see an Intune Wi-Fi profile, it pushes the network name and security settings to devices; the bands and channels are the access point's job.

Three bands, one trade-off

A band is a range of radio frequencies. The rule of thumb: lower frequency goes farther and through walls better; higher frequency is faster but shorter-range.[1][2]

BandRange and wallsSpeed and roomNotes
2.4 GHzLongest range, best through wallsSlowest, most crowdedShared with Bluetooth, microwaves, cordless gear
5 GHzShorter range, weakened by wallsFaster, many more channelsThe everyday workhorse band
6 GHzShortest rangeWidest channels, least crowdedNeeds Wi-Fi 6E or Wi-Fi 7 on both ends

The 6 GHz band was opened for unlicensed Wi-Fi in the US by the FCC in April 2020: 1,200 MHz of new spectrum (5.925–7.125 GHz), several times what Wi-Fi had before. Devices that can use it are branded Wi-Fi 6E.[3]

Channels and why 1, 6, 11

Each band is split into channels: slices of frequency an access point and its clients agree to use. Two nearby access points on the same or overlapping channels interfere, so you plan channels like seats in a room.

  • 2.4 GHz: the US allows channels 1 to 11 (other countries allow up to 13 or 14). The channels are spaced only 5 MHz apart, but each signal is about 20 MHz wide, so neighbors overlap. Only 1, 6 and 11 don't overlap each other.[2]
  • 5 GHz: about 25 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels, numbered 36 and up.[4]
  • 6 GHz: about 59 non-overlapping 20 MHz channels.[4]

Which channels are legal, and at what power, is set by each country's regulator (the FCC in the US). That's the "regulations" line in the exam objective: the same access point may offer different channels in different countries.[1]

Channel width: wider is faster, but fewer fit

Newer standards can bond neighboring 20 MHz channels into one wider channel: 40, 80 or 160 MHz (Wi-Fi 7 adds 320 MHz in 6 GHz). Twice the width carries roughly twice the data, but leaves fewer separate channels for neighbors.[4] In 5 GHz, 25 channels at 20 MHz become only about 2 at 160 MHz; in 6 GHz there are about 7. That's why wide channels live in 5 and 6 GHz, and 2.4 GHz stays at 20 MHz.

Memory hooks

Low = long, high = fast. 2.4 reaches, 5 and 6 race. 1, 6, 11: the only three 2.4 GHz channels that don't step on each other. Wider channel, fewer channels.

Try it (2 minutes)

On a Windows laptop connected to Wi-Fi, run this in a terminal:[5]

netsh wlan show interfaces

Find three lines: Radio type (the 802.11 standard in use), Band (2.4, 5 or 6 GHz) and Channel.

Radio type             : 802.11ax
Band                   : 5 GHz
Channel                : 44
Receive rate (Mbps)    : 1201
Signal                 : 88%

Your output will differ, and older Windows builds don't show the Band line: use the channel instead (1–14 means 2.4 GHz, 36 and up usually 5 GHz). Try it at your desk, then in the farthest room: did the band or the signal change?

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A user's laptop has full speed at their desk but is slow two rooms and a concrete wall away from the access point. What's the most likely explanation?

Higher frequencies carry more data but lose strength through walls. Far from the access point, a 5 GHz signal weakens (or the laptop falls back to 2.4 GHz), so speed drops. An expired lease or blocked port would break things outright, not just slow them.

You're placing three 2.4 GHz access points in a small office in the US. Which channel plan avoids overlap?

2.4 GHz channels sit 5 MHz apart but each is about 20 MHz wide, so only 1, 6 and 11 are clear of each other. Adjacent channels overlap, reusing one channel makes all three share it, and channel 12 isn't allowed in the US.

A new access point and laptop both support Wi-Fi 6E. Which band gives the widest, least crowded channels?

Wi-Fi 6E adds the 6 GHz band: 1,200 MHz of new spectrum with room for many wide channels and few older devices competing. 2.4 GHz is the most crowded, and 900 MHz isn't a regular Wi-Fi band.

An admin sets a busy 5 GHz access point to 160 MHz channels for top speed, and neighbors now interfere more. Why?

Bonding 20 MHz channels into 160 MHz leaves only about two 160 MHz channels in 5 GHz, so nearby access points end up sharing frequencies. Width doesn't change the band or anything at the IP layer.

From memory: name the three Wi-Fi bands and the trade-off between them, plus the three non-overlapping 2.4 GHz channels. (then click to check)

2.4, 5 and 6 GHz. Lower frequency reaches farther and through walls; higher is faster with more and wider channels but shorter range. Non-overlapping 2.4 GHz channels: 1, 6, 11.

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A mapped drive won't open after a firewall change, but the web works. Which port do you check first, and what's the legacy path it replaced? (lesson 0005, yesterday)

SMB on TCP 445. The legacy path was SMB over NetBIOS (137–139).

Which port does Remote Desktop use, and which port does HTTPS use? (lesson 0003, 3 days ago)

RDP: TCP 3389. HTTPS: TCP 443.

Name the four IP settings every device needs, and say what breaks if the DNS server is wrong. (lesson 0001, 5 days ago)

IP address, subnet mask, default gateway and DNS server. With a wrong DNS server, names don't resolve: sites fail by name even though pinging an IP address still works.
