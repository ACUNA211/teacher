Network tools: what's in the technician's bag

About 10 minutes. The last few lessons were about how networks are designed. Today is the hands-on side: the tools you use to build, find, test and watch cables and wireless links. Objective 2.8 lists eight of them.[1] The win: given a symptom, pick the right tool.

Why this matters at work

Not every "I can't sign in" ticket is about identity. If a desk's wired connection is dead, the PC can't reach the cloud sign-in service or check in with device management at all. Knowing which tool proves "the cable or port is bad" lets you hand the ticket to the right team quickly, without guessing.

Four jobs, eight tools

Group the tools by the job they do. It's easier to remember four jobs than eight names.

JobToolWhat it does
Build a cableCable stripperRemoves the outer jacket so you can reach the wires inside. Many crimpers have a stripper built in.[2]
CrimperSqueezes a connector onto the end of a cable, such as an RJ45 plug onto twisted pair: a patch cable.[1]
Punchdown toolPushes each wire into a patch panel, wall jack or punchdown block and trims the excess: the permanent end of a cable run.[1]
Find a cableTone generator and probe (toner probe)The generator puts a tone on one end of the wire; you wave the inductive probe over the bundle at the other end until it beeps.[1] It finds a wire; it doesn't test it.
Test a linkCable testerChecks every pin end to end: finds broken wires, missing pins and wires in the wrong order.[1]
Loopback plugWires the port's transmit pins back to its own receive pins, so a port can test itself with no cable or switch involved.[3]
Wi-Fi analyzerShows nearby networks, their signal strength and channels; used to plan, tune and troubleshoot coverage and find weak spots.[1]
Watch trafficNetwork tapSits inline on a link and sends an exact copy of the traffic to a monitoring port, while the real traffic passes through unchanged.[4]

Two pairs the exam likes to mix up

Crimper vs. punchdown tool. Ask: which end of the cable? A plug on a loose end (patch cable) → crimper. Wires seated into a panel or jack in the wall or closet → punchdown tool.

Toner probe vs. cable tester. Ask: do I know which cable it is? No → toner probe to find it. Yes, but is it good? → cable tester. A toner can beep happily on a cable with a broken pair.

A tap is different from a switch's port mirror (SPAN): with a mirror, the switch makes the copy itself and can drop packets when busy; a tap is separate hardware that copies everything on that one link.[4]

Memory hook

Build (strip, crimp, punch) → find (tone) → test (cable tester, loopback, Wi-Fi analyzer) → watch (tap). That's the order a cable's life goes in, too.

Try it (2 minutes)

Windows has a basic Wi-Fi analyzer built in. On a laptop with Wi-Fi, in PowerShell or Command Prompt:

netsh wlan show networks mode=bssid

Example output (trimmed; yours will differ):

SSID 1 : ExampleOffice
    Authentication          : WPA2-Personal
    BSSID 1                 : 02:00:5e:10:00:01
         Signal             : 86%
         Radio type         : 802.11ax
         Band               : 5 GHz
         Channel            : 36

Each BSSID is one access point radio. Look for the same network name on several BSSIDs, and for neighbors crowding your channel (lesson 0006). Walk to a spot with bad Wi-Fi and run it again: the signal percentage drop is your weak spot. On newer Windows 11 builds the command may ask you to turn on location services first.[5]

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

In a wiring closet, dozens of unlabeled cables end at a patch panel. You need to know which one runs to a particular desk's wall jack. What do you use?

Put the tone generator on the wall jack, then wave the probe over the patch panel until it beeps loudest. A cable tester checks a cable you've already identified; a crimper builds cables; a loopback plug tests a single port.

You're terminating new cable runs on the back of a patch panel in the wiring closet. Which tool seats each wire into the panel?

Patch panels, wall jacks and punchdown blocks are the permanent ends of a run: the punchdown tool pushes each wire into its slot and trims it. A crimper puts a plug on a loose cable end (a patch cable) instead.

A desktop shows no link light. A known-good cable plugged into a known-good switch port changes nothing. Which tool tests the PC's network port by itself?

A loopback plug sends the port's own transmit signal straight back into its receive pins, so you test the network card with nothing else in the path. You've already ruled out the cable and the switch, so the port is the last suspect.

Users in one corner of an office keep dropping off Wi-Fi; elsewhere it's fine. Which tool shows the signal strength and channels right at that spot?

A Wi-Fi analyzer measures what the radio actually hears in that place: signal strength, which networks are there, and which channels they use. That shows a coverage gap or a crowded channel. The other three tools work on cables and ports, not radio.

From memory: name the four jobs and the eight tools that do them. (then click to check)

Build: cable stripper, crimper, punchdown tool. Find: tone generator and probe. Test: cable tester, loopback plug, Wi-Fi analyzer. Watch: network tap.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

Which internet connection type is limited by distance from the provider's central office, and which one needs line of sight to a tower? (lesson 0018, yesterday)

DSL runs over phone lines and gets slower the farther you are from the central office (roughly a 10,000-foot limit). A WISP (fixed wireless) needs clear line of sight from the rooftop antenna to the tower.

With a VPN, what's the difference between a full tunnel and a split tunnel? (lesson 0016, 3 days ago)

Full tunnel: all traffic goes through the VPN to the company network, even internet browsing. Split tunnel: only traffic for company networks goes through the VPN; everything else goes straight to the internet.

Which DNS record type points a name at another name, and which one tells the world where to deliver a domain's email? (lesson 0012, 7 days ago)

A CNAME is an alias: it points one name at another name. An MX record names the mail server for the domain; when there are several, the lowest preference number is tried first.
