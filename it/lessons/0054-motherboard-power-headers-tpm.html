Power, ports and pins: motherboard connections

About 10 minutes. Yesterday was the board's size. Today is what gets plugged into it from inside the case. Messer's 3.5 video groups these into three kinds: power connections, storage drive interfaces and header pins, and his example board has headers for USB and a TPM.[1] The win: look at a cable inside a PC and know where it goes, and why a missing one gives a "dead" PC.

Why this matters at work

The TPM is what Windows 11 requires, what BitLocker stores its keys in, and what device-compliance checks (for example in Intune) often rely on. When a PC "has no TPM", the usual cause is not a missing chip: it's a firmware setting that is turned off.[4]

1. Power connections

ConnectorPowersNotes
24-pin ATX (often 20+4)The motherboard itselfThe biggest plug on the board. The 4 extra pins snap off so the cable also fits older 20-pin boards.[2]
4- or 8-pin CPU (EPS12V, often 4+4)The processorNear the CPU socket, usually top-left. Forget it and the PC usually won't start or show anything.
6- or 8-pin PCIe (6+2)Graphics cardPlugs into the card, not the board. Looks like the CPU plug but is wired differently: don't swap them.[2]

SATA power (15-pin) and Molex go to drives and were covered in lesson 0042 and lesson 0045. Each plug is keyed (shaped so it fits only one way). If it won't go in, it's the wrong plug or it's the wrong way round. Don't force it.

2. Storage drive interfaces

You already know these from objective 3.4: SATA ports (small L-shaped 7-pin data ports, often in a row at the board's edge) and M.2 slots (lying flat on the board, often under a heatsink).[1] Quick recap: SATA cables, M.2 keys and lengths.

3. Header pins

A header is a small block of pins on the board that a case cable or add-on plugs onto.

HeaderWhat plugs inLook for
Front panel (F_PANEL, JFP1)Case power button, reset button, power LED, drive activity LEDTiny 2-pin plugs. LEDs have polarity (+/−); switches don't.[3]
USB 2.0 (9-pin)Front USB-A ports, card readers2×5 block with one pin missing as a key; 480 Mbps[5]
USB 3.x (19-pin)Two front USB-A 5 Gbps portsLarger, usually blue, thick cable[5]
Front USB-C (20-pin "Key-A" / Type-E)A front USB-C portSmall and latched; not the same as the 19-pin header[5]
TPMA plug-in discrete TPM moduleOften empty, because most modern CPUs have a firmware TPM built in: Intel PTT, AMD fTPM[4]
Fan / pump (CPU_FAN, SYS_FAN)Case and CPU cooler fans3-pin (voltage control) or 4-pin (PWM speed control)
Rule of thumb

Two power plugs to the board: 24-pin main + CPU 4/8-pin. Headers bring the case's buttons, LEDs and front ports to the board. Power button does nothing on a new build? Check the front panel header and the CPU power plug first. Pin layouts differ by board: the manual has the map.

Try it (2 minutes)

  1. On a Windows PC, open PowerShell as administrator and run Get-Tpm.
  2. Look at TpmPresent (Windows sees a TPM) and TpmReady (it's set up and usable). Both should be True.[4]
  3. For the TPM version, run tpm.msc and read Specification Version (Windows 11 needs 2.0).
Before checking: TpmPresent is False on a PC with a recent CPU. What's the most likely cause, and where do you fix it? (then click to check)

The firmware TPM is turned off in UEFI/BIOS (a setting called something like Intel PTT, AMD fTPM or Security Device Support). Turn it on there. You rarely need to buy a module for the TPM header.[4]

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A new build: fans spin when the PSU switch is flipped on, but there's no display and the CPU seems dead. The 24-pin is connected. What's the most likely missing plug?

The board gets power from the 24-pin, but the processor gets its own feed from the 4/8-pin CPU (EPS12V) connector near the socket. Without it, the CPU doesn't run. SATA power only feeds drives; PCIe power feeds a graphics card.

After moving a PC into a new case, the case's power button does nothing, though the PSU is on and all power cables are connected. Where do you look first?

The case button reaches the board through a tiny 2-pin plug on the front panel header. A common mistake is putting it on the reset or LED pins. Check the board manual's pin map.[3]

A user's new case has a front USB-C port that doesn't work. The board has free 9-pin and 19-pin USB headers only. What's the issue?

A front USB-C port uses its own 20-pin Key-A / Type-E header, which is different from the 19-pin USB 3.x header.[5] Without it, the port can't be connected directly (an adapter may work, at lower capability).

A PC fails the Windows 11 check for TPM 2.0. The board has an empty TPM header and a recent Intel CPU. What do you try first?

Recent Intel CPUs include a firmware TPM 2.0 called PTT (AMD's is fTPM), and Windows 11 accepts it just like a discrete chip. It's often off by default in UEFI.[4] The header is only needed for a discrete module.

From memory: the two power plugs every modern board needs, and four kinds of header pins. (then click to check)

24-pin ATX (board) and 4/8-pin CPU (processor). Headers: front panel (power/reset/LEDs), USB (9-pin 2.0, 19-pin 3.x, 20-pin USB-C), TPM, fan.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A case lists "mATX / Mini-ITX" support. Will an ATX board fit? Will a Mini-ITX board? (lesson 0053, 1 day ago)

ATX: no, too big. Mini-ITX: yes. Smaller boards reuse a subset of the bigger boards' mounting holes, so small board → big case works; big board → small case doesn't.

A desktop takes DDR4. Will a DDR5 stick fit in its slot? How can you tell the generations apart? (lesson 0047, 7 days ago)

No. DDR4 and DDR5 DIMMs both have 288 pins, but the notch sits in a different place, so a stick only fits its own generation. Check the board's spec (or Win32_PhysicalMemory SMBIOSMemoryType: 26 DDR4, 34 DDR5).

A user wants to block their laptop's camera for privacy, but Windows Hello face sign-in stops working. Why? (lesson 0024, 30 days ago)

Windows Hello face recognition needs the IR camera. Covering or disabling the camera hardware (a shutter, a privacy key, or a policy that blocks the camera) can stop Hello face too. Offer PIN or fingerprint instead.
