Power, modems and the NIC: the ends of the cable

About 10 minutes. Yesterday covered the boxes in the middle of the network. Today finishes objective 2.5 with the two ends: where the provider's line comes in (cable modem, DSL modem, ONT), where each device plugs in (the NIC), and how a cable can carry power as well as data (PoE).[1] The win: look at a wall of gear and say what each box at the edge does.

Why this matters at work

Device management and sign-in records identify hardware partly by its network adapter, and Wi-Fi and wired adapters on the same laptop have different MAC addresses. When a DHCP reservation or an inventory record "doesn't match", the first question is which adapter the device used. And when a desk phone or access point goes dark while the PC beside it still works, think power before network: is its PoE switch port still delivering?

Power over Ethernet (PoE)

PoE sends DC power down the same Ethernet cable as the data, so an access point on a ceiling or a camera on a wall needs no outlet nearby.[1] The power can come from two places:

  • PoE switch (called endspan): the switch port itself supplies power. One box powers many devices.
  • PoE injector (called midspan): a small box placed between an ordinary switch and one device. Handy when the switch can't do PoE.[1][2]
StandardNicknameSent by the portGuaranteed at the device
802.3afPoE15.4 W12.95 W
802.3atPoE+30 W25.5 W
802.3bt Type 3PoE++60 W51 W
802.3bt Type 4PoE++100 W71.3 W

Some power is lost as heat in the cable, so the device gets less than the port sends.[3] Study guides often quote a mix (15.4, 25.5, 51, 71.3 W);[1] learn the order af → at → bt and that each step roughly doubles. Newer standards still power older devices.[3]

Where the provider's line comes in

Each internet connection from lesson 0018 ends in its own box, which hands you plain Ethernet for your router.

BoxLine it takesNotes
Cable modemCoax from the cable-TV providerSpeaks DOCSIS; data shares the cable with TV channels.[1]
DSL modemTelephone lineUsually asymmetric (ADSL): download faster than upload.[1]
ONT (optical network terminal)FiberConverts light to electrical Ethernet. It's the demarcation point: the provider owns everything up to and including it; what you plug into it is yours.[1][4]

The demarc idea matters for tickets: if the fault is on the provider's side of it, you call the provider; on your side, it's yours to fix.

The NIC: every device has one

The network interface card is the most basic network device: anything that talks on a network has one, wired or wireless, built in or added as a card or USB adapter.[1] Each NIC carries its own hardware address, the MAC address, which is what switches and access points decide by (yesterday's lesson). A laptop with Ethernet, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth has three NICs and three MACs.

Memory hook

Coax → cable modem. Phone line → DSL modem. Fiber → ONT (the demarc). PoE: af, at, bt; about 15, 30, 60–100 W. Switch = endspan, injector = midspan. One NIC, one MAC.

Try it (2 minutes)

List every NIC on your PC with its MAC and speed.[5] In PowerShell:

Get-NetAdapter | Format-Table Name, InterfaceDescription, MacAddress, LinkSpeed, Status

Example output (trimmed; yours will differ):

Name      InterfaceDescription            MacAddress         LinkSpeed Status
----      --------------------            ----------         --------- ------
Ethernet  Realtek PCIe GbE Family Ctrl    02-00-5E-10-00-0A  1 Gbps    Up
Wi-Fi     Intel(R) Wi-Fi 6 AX201          02-00-5E-10-00-0B  866.7 Mbps Up

Two adapters, two MACs. A Wi-Fi adapter may also use a random MAC per network (a privacy feature), which is another reason a reservation can miss.

Practice

Answer from memory before scrolling back up.

A new ceiling access point must go where there's no power outlet. The closet switch has no PoE, and buying a new switch isn't approved. What's the cheapest fix?

An injector (midspan) sits between a non-PoE switch and one device and adds power to that one cable. A PoE switch (endspan) would also work but was ruled out. Patch panels are passive and never supply power.

A pan-tilt camera needs about 50 W at the device. Which is the oldest PoE standard that can deliver that?

802.3af guarantees about 13 W at the device and 802.3at about 25.5 W, both too little. 802.3bt Type 3 guarantees 51 W. 802.11ax is a Wi-Fi standard, not a power standard.

A branch office on fiber has no internet. Your router shows no link on its WAN port, and the provider's box on the wall has a red alarm light. Who do you call?

The ONT is the demarcation point. A fault light on it means the problem is on the provider's side of the demarc, so the provider owns the fix. If the ONT looked healthy and only your router failed, it would be yours.

A laptop has a DHCP reservation, but it gets a random address from the pool today. It's docked with Wi-Fi off. What's the most likely reason?

Reservations match one MAC address, and every NIC has its own. A reservation made for the Wi-Fi adapter won't match the dock's Ethernet NIC, so the laptop gets a normal pool address. The ONT, PoE and DSL don't take part in handing out addresses.

From memory: which box terminates coax, a phone line and fiber? And what's the difference between an endspan and a midspan? (then click to check)

Coax: cable modem (DOCSIS). Phone line: DSL modem. Fiber: ONT, the demarcation point. Endspan: the PoE switch powers the port itself. Midspan: an injector adds power between a normal switch and one device.

Review: earlier lessons

Spaced retrieval: pull these from memory first, then check.

A switch, a router and a firewall each make forwarding decisions. What does each one look at? (lesson 0020, yesterday)

Switch: the MAC address (same network). Router: the IP address (between networks; your default gateway). Firewall: its rules, such as port numbers or applications.

A PC has an 8-day DHCP lease. On which day does it first try to renew, who does it ask, and what happens at 87.5%? (lesson 0014, 7 days ago)

At 50% (T1), day 4, it asks the same server that gave the lease. If that server never answers, at 87.5% (T2), day 7, it broadcasts to any DHCP server.

Match 802.11n, ac, ax and be to their Wi-Fi numbers. Which of them is 5 GHz only? (lesson 0007, 14 days ago)

n = Wi-Fi 4, ac = Wi-Fi 5, ax = Wi-Fi 6 (6E adds 6 GHz), be = Wi-Fi 7. 802.11ac is the 5 GHz-only one.
